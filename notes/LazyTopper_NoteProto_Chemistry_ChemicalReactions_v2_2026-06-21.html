Chemical Reactions & Equations

★

What the board actually asks: balance a skeletal equation (with state symbols), identify the reaction type (combination / decomposition / displacement / double-displacement / redox), and explain everyday effects — corrosion and rancidity. The equation is the answer here.

The Big Idea

New substances, written as a balanced equation.

A chemical reaction makes new substances — you spot one by a change in state, colour, temperature, or a gas. We write it as an equation, then balance it so atoms are conserved. Almost every reaction in this chapter sorts into one of five types, and many are also redox — oxygen or hydrogen changing hands.

Definitions the board rewards

State these exactly

These short definitions are 1-mark answers — say them the way the textbook does.

Balanced chemical equation
An equation in which the number of atoms of each element is the same on both sides — because mass can neither be created nor destroyed (law of conservation of mass).
Combination reaction
A reaction in which a single product is formed from two or more reactants.
Decomposition reaction
A reaction in which a single reactant breaks down to give two or more simpler products.
Displacement reaction
A reaction in which a more reactive element displaces another element from its compound.
Double-displacement reaction
A reaction in which there is an exchange of ions between the reactants (a precipitate often forms).
Oxidation & reduction
Oxidation = gain of oxygen or loss of hydrogen. Reduction = loss of oxygen or gain of hydrogen. A reaction with both is a redox reaction.
Exothermic / endothermic
Exothermic: heat is released with the products. Endothermic: energy is absorbed.
Must-know concepts

…and where they’re tested

1
Writing & balancing equations
Balance by the hit-and-trial method — never change a formula, only the coefficients. Add state symbols $(s),(l),(g),(aq)$ to make it informative.
3 mark · balance + states
2
Combination reactions
Two or more reactants → one product, e.g. $\text{CaO}+\text{H}_2\text{O}\rightarrow\text{Ca(OH)}_2$. Usually exothermic (heat released).
Identify + example
3
Decomposition reactions
One reactant → simpler products, driven by heat (thermal), light (photolytic), or electricity (electrolytic). Usually endothermic.
Energy-source classification
4
Displacement reactions
A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive one: $\text{Fe}+\text{CuSO}_4\rightarrow\text{FeSO}_4+\text{Cu}$. Iron nail browns; blue solution fades.
Reactivity + colour change
5
Double-displacement & precipitation
Ions swap partners; an insoluble salt drops out as a precipitate: $\text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4+\text{BaCl}_2\rightarrow\text{BaSO}_4\!\downarrow+2\text{NaCl}$.
Name the precipitate
6
Oxidation–reduction (redox)
One reactant is oxidised while the other is reduced: $\text{CuO}+\text{H}_2\rightarrow\text{Cu}+\text{H}_2\text{O}$ — CuO loses O (reduced), H₂ gains O (oxidised).
Which is oxidised / reduced
7
Effects in everyday life
Corrosion: a metal is attacked by moisture/acids (rusting of iron). Rancidity: fats/oils oxidise, smell and taste change — slowed by antioxidants or flushing with nitrogen.
2 mark · define + example
Worked examples — by reaction shape

One example per question-shape

BalancingNCERT §1.1.2 · 3 mk▸
Balance the skeletal equation: $\text{Fe}+\text{H}_2\text{O}\rightarrow\text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4+\text{H}_2$
1
Count atoms. LHS Fe 1, H 2, O 1; RHS Fe 3, H 2, O 4 — unbalanced.
2
Balance the biggest formula. $\text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4$ has 4 O → put 4 before $\text{H}_2\text{O}$. start with most atoms
3
Balance H. 8 H on the left now → put 4 before $\text{H}_2$.
4
Balance Fe. 3 Fe on the right → put 3 before Fe. Final: $3\text{Fe}+4\text{H}_2\text{O}\rightarrow\text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4+4\text{H}_2$.
Mark scheme: correct coefficients (2) + verification that atoms match both sides (1). Never alter a formula — only coefficients.
CombinationNCERT Activity 1.4 · 2 mk▸
What happens when quicklime is added to water? Write the equation and classify it.
1
Equation. $\text{CaO}(s)+\text{H}_2\text{O}(l)\rightarrow\text{Ca(OH)}_2(aq)+\text{Heat}$.
2
Type. Two reactants → one product: a combination reaction. combination
3
Energy. Heat is released, so it is also exothermic. exothermic
Mark scheme: balanced equation (1) + both classifications, combination + exothermic (1).
Double displacementNCERT Activity 1.10 · 2 mk▸
Sodium sulphate solution is mixed with barium chloride solution. What is observed, and what is the equation?
1
Observation. A white insoluble substance — a precipitate of barium sulphate — forms.
2
Equation. $\text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4(aq)+\text{BaCl}_2(aq)\rightarrow\text{BaSO}_4(s)\!\downarrow+2\text{NaCl}(aq)$.
3
Type. Ions exchange partners → double-displacement (precipitation). double displacement
Mark scheme: name the precipitate $\text{BaSO}_4$ (1) + balanced equation with states (1).
RedoxNCERT §1.2.5 · 2 mk▸
In $\text{CuO}+\text{H}_2\rightarrow\text{Cu}+\text{H}_2\text{O}$, which substance is oxidised and which is reduced?
1
CuO. Loses oxygen → it is reduced to Cu. reduction = loss of O
2
H₂. Gains oxygen → it is oxidised to $\text{H}_2\text{O}$. oxidation = gain of O
3
Name. Both happen together → a redox reaction. redox
Mark scheme: CuO reduced (1) + H₂ oxidised (1). State the rule — gain of O is oxidation, loss of O is reduction.
Strategy

How to attack a chemical-reaction question

→
Balance before anything else. Start with the compound that has the most atoms; adjust coefficients only.
→
Classify by counting reactants/products. One product → combination; one reactant → decomposition; element swap → displacement; ion swap → double-displacement.
→
For redox, track the oxygen/hydrogen. Whoever gains O (or loses H) is oxidised; the other is reduced.
→
Add state symbols + conditions $(s),(l),(g),(aq)$ and Heat/Light/Electricity over the arrow when asked for a “complete” equation.
Pitfalls

Where students lose easy marks

✕
Changing a formula to balance (writing $\text{H}_2\text{O}_4$ instead of $4\text{H}_2\text{O}$). Only coefficients may change.
✕
Forgetting state symbols when the question asks for a complete equation.
✕
Confusing displacement (one element swaps) with double-displacement (ions swap).
✕
Reversing oxidation and reduction — remember OIL RIG via oxygen: gain O = oxidation.
✕
Calling every precipitation reaction “combination.” A precipitate means double-displacement.
