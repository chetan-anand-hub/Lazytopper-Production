Light — Reflection & Refraction

The Big Idea AUTHORED

Light bends and bounces — predictably.

Two behaviours explain this whole chapter. Light reflects off mirrors (bouncing back) and refracts through lenses and other media (bending as it changes speed). Once you can trace a couple of standard rays, you can predict exactly where an image forms, how big it is, and whether it's real or virtual — and the mirror/lens formula turns that into numbers.

Definitions the board rewards NCERT VERBATIM

State these in NCERT's exact words

CBSE rewards the textbook's precise wording for 1-mark definitions. Each quote below is reproduced verbatim from NCERT with its page — memorise the quote, not a paraphrase. The plain-English line after is only to help you understand it.

Laws of reflection of light
📖 NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording · Ch 9, p.135
"(i) The angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection, and (ii) The incident ray, the normal to the mirror at the point of incidence and the reflected ray, all lie in the same plane."
What it means: the ray comes in and bounces off at the same angle to the normal ($\angle i = \angle r$), and the incident ray, normal and reflected ray are all flat in one plane.
Laws of refraction of light (Snell's law)
📖 NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording · Ch 9, p.148
"(i) The incident ray, the refracted ray and the normal to the interface of two transparent media at the point of incidence, all lie in the same plane. (ii) The ratio of sine of angle of incidence to the sine of angle of refraction is a constant, for the light of a given colour and for the given pair of media. This law is also known as Snell's law of refraction."
What it means: $\dfrac{\sin i}{\sin r}$ stays the same for a given colour of light and a given pair of media — that constant is the refractive index.
Absolute refractive index
📖 NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording · Ch 9, p.148–149
"If medium 1 is vacuum or air, then the refractive index of medium 2 is considered with respect to vacuum. This is called the absolute refractive index of the medium."
What it means: measured against vacuum/air, $n_m = \dfrac{\text{Speed of light in air}}{\text{Speed of light in the medium}} = \dfrac{c}{v}$ (NCERT p.149). A larger $n$ means light slows more — the medium is optically denser.
Principal focus & focal length of a spherical mirror
📖 NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording · Ch 9, p.136
"[Rays parallel to the principal axis] are all meeting/intersecting at a point on the principal axis of the mirror. This point is called the principal focus of the concave mirror. … The distance between the pole and the principal focus of a spherical mirror is called the focal length."
What it means: parallel rays converge at F (concave) — focal length $f$ = pole-to-F distance, and $R = 2f$.
Magnification produced by a spherical mirror
📖 NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording · Ch 9, p.143
"Magnification produced by a spherical mirror gives the relative extent to which the image of an object is magnified with respect to the object size. It is expressed as the ratio of the height of the image to the height of the object."
What it means: $m = \dfrac{h'}{h} = -\dfrac{v}{u}$. A negative $m$ means a real, inverted image; positive means virtual, erect.
Power of a lens & the dioptre
📖 NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording · Ch 9, p.158
"The power of a lens is defined as the reciprocal of its focal length. … The SI unit of power of a lens is 'dioptre'. It is denoted by the letter D. … 1 dioptre is the power of a lens whose focal length is 1 metre. 1D = 1 m⁻¹."
What it means: $P = \dfrac{1}{f\,(\text{metres})}$. Convex lens → positive power, concave → negative.
📖 Key terms — NCERT verbatim (state word-for-word) · Ch 9, p.135–151
Pole
"The centre of the reflecting surface of a spherical mirror is a point called the pole." (p.135)
Centre of curvature
"…This point is called the centre of curvature of the spherical mirror." (p.136)
Radius of curvature
"The radius of the sphere of which the reflecting surface of a spherical mirror forms a part, is called the radius of curvature of the mirror." (p.136)
Principal axis
"[A straight line passing through the pole and the centre of curvature…] This line is called the principal axis." (p.136)
Aperture
"The diameter of the reflecting surface of spherical mirror is called its aperture." (p.137)
R = 2f
"…the radius of curvature is found to be equal to twice the focal length. We put this as R = 2f." (p.137)
Lens
"A transparent material bound by two surfaces, of which one or both surfaces are spherical, forms a lens." (p.150)
Optical centre
"The central point of a lens is its optical centre. … A ray of light through the optical centre of a lens passes without suffering any deviation." (p.150–151)

Mirror formula & magnification

Refraction & refractive index
Light bends toward the normal entering a denser medium. $n = \dfrac{c}{v} = \dfrac{\sin i}{\sin r}$.
2–3 marks
4
Apparent depth & everyday refraction
Because light bends when leaving a denser medium, objects in water look shallower / raised and a pencil looks bent. The relation: $\;n = \dfrac{\text{real depth}}{\text{apparent depth}}$. Real depth is always greater than apparent depth.

Lens formula, magnification & power
$\dfrac{1}{v} - \dfrac{1}{u} = \dfrac{1}{f}$, $\;m = \dfrac{v}{u}$, and power $P = \dfrac{1}{f\,(\text{metres})}$ in dioptres (D).

Concave mirror · NCERT Example 9.2p.144▸
"An object, 4.0 cm in size, is placed at 25.0 cm in front of a concave mirror of focal length 15.0 cm. At what distance from the mirror should a screen be placed in order to obtain a sharp image? Find the nature and the size of the image."
1
List with signs (NCERT). $h = +4.0$ cm, $u = -25.0$ cm, $f = -15.0$ cm.Concept: New Cartesian signs
2
Mirror formula. $\dfrac{1}{v} = \dfrac{1}{f} - \dfrac{1}{u} = \dfrac{1}{-15.0} - \dfrac{1}{-25.0} = \dfrac{-5.0+3.0}{75.0} = \dfrac{-2.0}{75.0}$.Concept: substitute with signs
3
Solve. $v = -37.5$ cm → the screen should be placed 37.5 cm in front of the mirror; the image is real.Concept: sign tells nature
4
Size. $h' = -\dfrac{v\,h}{u} = -\dfrac{(-37.5)(4.0)}{(-25.0)} = -6.0$ cm → the image is inverted and enlarged.Concept: h′ sign + size
Mark-scheme logic (authored): 1 for signed data, 1 for formula + solving $v$, 1 for nature + size with sign. Stating "real, inverted, enlarged" is where the last mark sits.
Convex lens · NCERT Example 9.4p.156▸
"A 2.0 cm tall object is placed perpendicular to the principal axis of a convex lens of focal length 10 cm. The distance of the object from the lens is 15 cm. Find the nature, position and size of the image. Also find its magnification."
1
List with signs (NCERT). $h = +2.0$ cm, $f = +10$ cm, $u = -15$ cm.Concept: lens sign convention
2
Lens formula. $\dfrac{1}{v} = \dfrac{1}{u} + \dfrac{1}{f} = \dfrac{1}{-15} + \dfrac{1}{10} = \dfrac{-2+3}{30} = \dfrac{1}{30}$.Concept: substitute
3
Solve. $v = +30$ cm → image is 30 cm on the other side of the lens; real and inverted.Concept: read the sign
4
Magnification & size. $m = \dfrac{v}{u} = \dfrac{30}{-15} = -2$; $\;h' = h\dfrac{v}{u} = -4.0$ cm → real, inverted, two times enlarged.Concept: m for lens
Mark-scheme logic (authored): 1 signs · 1 formula + solve · 1 nature/size. Lens magnification is $+v/u$ (no minus, unlike the mirror's $m=-v/u$).
Refractive index · NCERT in-text questionp.150, Q2▸
"Light enters from air to glass having refractive index 1.50. What is the speed of light in the glass? The speed of light in vacuum is $3 \times 10^8$ m s⁻¹."
1
Use the definition. $n = \dfrac{c}{v} \Rightarrow v = \dfrac{c}{n}$.Concept: absolute refractive index
2
Substitute. $v = \dfrac{3\times10^8}{1.50} = \mathbf{2\times10^8}$ m s⁻¹.Concept: compute with unit

Power of a lens · NCERT in-text questionp.158, Q3▸
"Find the power of a concave lens of focal length 2 m."
1
Sign + units. Concave lens → $f = -2$ m (already in metres).Concept: sign of f
2
Power. $P = \dfrac{1}{f} = \dfrac{1}{-2} = \mathbf{-0.5\ \text{D}}$. The negative sign confirms it is a concave (diverging) lens.Concept: power in dioptres
Mark-scheme logic (authored): 1 for $f$ in metres with the correct sign, 1 for the answer with unit D. Forgetting the sign (or leaving $f$ in cm) is the classic slip.
Problem-solving strategies AUTHORED

How to attack any Light question

→
Numerical? Write down $u, v, f$ with signs before touching the formula. Most marks are lost at the sign step, not the algebra.
→
"Draw the ray diagram"? Use any two of the three standard rays; the image is where the reflected/refracted rays meet.
→
Mirror vs lens magnification? Mirror $m=-v/u$; lens $m=+v/u$. Don't mix them.
→
Power question? Convert $f$ to metres first, then $P=1/f$ in dioptres.
Common mistakes that cost marks AUTHORED · real CBSE pitfalls

Where students lose easy marks

✕
Sign convention errors — the #1 lost mark. Object distance $u$ is always negative; concave $f$ negative, convex $f$ positive.
✕
Mixing mirror and lens magnification — mirror $m=-v/u$, lens $m=+v/u$.
✕
Forgetting to convert to metres for power — $P=1/f$ needs $f$ in metres, not cm.
✕
Not stating the nature — a numerical answer needs "real/virtual, erect/inverted, magnified/diminished" for full marks.

Rules & Conventions

The New Cartesian Sign Convention

CBSE does not require the mirror/lens formula derivation for this chapter — so the formal core here isn't a proof, it's the sign convention and ray rules that make every numerical and diagram correct. This is the "Proof" tab, adapted to what the topic actually has.

NCERT Fig 9.9The New Cartesian Sign Convention for spherical mirrors
All distances are measured from the pole P. The object is always on the left, so the incident light travels left→right: distances along the incident light (to the right) are +, against it (to the left) are −; heights above the axis are +, below are −.

Distances (along principal axis)

  • •All distances measured from the pole / optical centre.
  • −Distances against the incident light (object side) are negative.
  • +Distances along the incident light are positive.

What this means in practice

  • −Object distance $u$ is always negative (real object).
  • −Concave mirror / concave lens focal length: negative.
  • +Convex mirror / convex lens focal length: positive.
  • ±Heights above axis +, below axis −.
Ray-diagram construction

The three standard rays (use any two)

1
A ray parallel to the principal axis reflects/refracts through the principal focus F.
2
A ray through the focus F reflects/refracts parallel to the principal axis.
3
A ray through the centre of curvature C (mirror) / optical centre O (lens) returns along its path / passes undeviated.
