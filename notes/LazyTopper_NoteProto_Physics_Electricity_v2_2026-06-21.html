Electricity

★

What the board actually asks: Ohm’s-law numericals, the resistance–resistivity relation $R=\rho l/A$, equivalent resistance in series & parallel, and the heating effect ($H=I^2Rt$) with electric power ($P=VI$). Almost every mark sits in a numerical.

The Big Idea

Charge flows; resistance fights back.

A cell sets up a potential difference that pushes charge through a conductor — that flow is the current. Every conductor resists that flow, and Ohm’s law ties the three together: $V=IR$. Once you can find the equivalent resistance of a network, everything else — the current, the power, the heat — follows from $V$, $I$ and $R$.

Definitions the board rewards

State these exactly

One-mark questions ask for these word-for-word — say them the way the textbook does.

Electric current
The rate of flow of electric charge through a cross-section: $I=\dfrac{Q}{t}$. SI unit: ampere (A); $1\,\text{A}=1\,\text{C/s}$.
Potential difference
The work done to move a unit charge from one point to the other: $V=\dfrac{W}{Q}$. SI unit: volt (V); $1\,\text{V}=1\,\text{J/C}$.
Ohm’s law
The potential difference across a metallic conductor is directly proportional to the current through it, provided the temperature stays constant: $V\propto I$, so $V=IR$.
Resistance
The property of a conductor to oppose the flow of charge: $R=\dfrac{V}{I}$. SI unit: ohm ($\Omega$); $1\,\Omega=1\,\text{V/A}$.
Resistivity ($\rho$)
A characteristic property of the material in $R=\rho\dfrac{l}{A}$. SI unit: $\Omega\,\text{m}$. Independent of the wire’s shape or size.
Electric power
The rate at which electrical energy is consumed: $P=VI=I^2R=\dfrac{V^2}{R}$. SI unit: watt (W).
Must-know concepts

…and where they’re tested

1
Ohm’s law & the V–I graph
For a metallic conductor at fixed temperature, $V/I$ is constant. The graph of $V$ against $I$ is a straight line through the origin — its slope is the resistance $R$.
2 mark · graph + slope = R
NCERT Fig 11.3 (authored)Ohm’s law: V–I is a straight line through O
2
Factors affecting resistance
$R\propto l$ and $R\propto \dfrac{1}{A}$, giving $R=\rho\dfrac{l}{A}$. Longer wire → more resistance; thicker wire → less.
Derivation · R ∝ l/A numerical
3
Resistivity — material property
Conductors $\sim 10^{-8}\,\Omega\text{m}$, insulators $\sim 10^{12}\,\Omega\text{m}$. Alloys (nichrome, manganin) have higher resistivity and don’t oxidise — used in heating elements; tungsten for bulb filaments.
Why alloys for heaters
4
Resistors in series
Same current through each; $R_s=R_1+R_2+R_3$ — always larger than any single resistor.
2–3 mark equivalent-R
NCERT Fig 11.6 (authored)Resistors in series — one current path
5
Resistors in parallel
Same voltage across each; $\dfrac{1}{R_p}=\dfrac{1}{R_1}+\dfrac{1}{R_2}+\dfrac{1}{R_3}$ — always smaller than any single resistor. Domestic wiring is parallel.
2–3 mark equivalent-R
NCERT Fig 11.7 (authored)Resistors in parallel — current splits
6
Heating effect (Joule’s law)
Heat $H=I^2Rt$ — proportional to $I^2$, to $R$, and to time. Used in the heater, the electric iron, and the fuse (melts on excess current).
3 mark · H = I²Rt
7
Electric power & energy
$P=VI=I^2R=\dfrac{V^2}{R}$. Commercial energy unit is the kilowatt-hour: $1\,\text{kWh}=3.6\times10^6\,\text{J}$.
Power rating · cost numerical
Worked examples — numericals by shape

One example per question-shape

Physics rule: write the given quantities with units at step 1, then the formula, then substitute. Sourced from NCERT Examples 11.2, 11.3, 11.8, 11.10.

Charge / workNCERT Ex 11.2 · 1–2 mk▸
How much work is done in moving a charge of 2 C across two points having a potential difference of 12 V?
1
Given. $Q=2\,\text{C}$, $V=12\,\text{V}$, $W=?$
2
Formula. $V=\dfrac{W}{Q}\Rightarrow W=VQ$. definition of V
3
Substitute. $W=12\,\text{V}\times 2\,\text{C}=24\,\text{J}$.
Mark scheme: formula (½) + correct substitution with units (½) + answer 24 J with unit (1).
Ohm’s lawNCERT Ex 11.3 · 2 mk▸
How much current will an electric bulb draw from a 220 V source if the resistance of the filament is 1200 Ω?
1
Given. $V=220\,\text{V}$, $R=1200\,\Omega$, $I=?$
2
Formula. From Ohm’s law $I=\dfrac{V}{R}$. V = IR
3
Substitute. $I=\dfrac{220}{1200}=0.18\,\text{A}$.
Mark scheme: correct rearrangement (1) + answer 0.18 A with unit (1).
Parallel networkNCERT Ex 11.8 · 3 mk▸
$R_1=5\,\Omega$, $R_2=10\,\Omega$, $R_3=30\,\Omega$ are connected in parallel to a 12 V battery. Find the current in each, the total current, and the total resistance.
1
Same voltage. In parallel, $V=12\,\text{V}$ across every resistor. parallel rule
2
Branch currents. $I_1=\tfrac{12}{5}=2.4$, $I_2=\tfrac{12}{10}=1.2$, $I_3=\tfrac{12}{30}=0.4$ A. I = V/R
3
Total current. $I=2.4+1.2+0.4=4\,\text{A}$. I = I₁+I₂+I₃
4
Equivalent R. $\tfrac{1}{R_p}=\tfrac{1}{5}+\tfrac{1}{10}+\tfrac{1}{30}=\tfrac{1}{3}\Rightarrow R_p=3\,\Omega$.
Mark scheme: branch currents (1) + total current (1) + $R_p=3\,\Omega$ (1). Note $R_p$ is smaller than the smallest resistor — a quick sanity check.
Power ratingNCERT Ex 11.10 · 3 mk▸
An electric iron consumes 840 W at maximum and 360 W at minimum, on 220 V. Find the current and resistance in each case.
1
Formula. $P=VI\Rightarrow I=\dfrac{P}{V}$; then $R=\dfrac{V}{I}$. P = VI
2
Maximum. $I=\tfrac{840}{220}=3.82\,\text{A}$, $R=\tfrac{220}{3.82}=57.6\,\Omega$.
3
Minimum. $I=\tfrac{360}{220}=1.64\,\text{A}$, $R=\tfrac{220}{1.64}=134.2\,\Omega$.
Mark scheme: each case current (½) + resistance (1) × 2 = 3. Lower power → higher resistance on the same supply.
Strategy

How to attack any Electricity numerical

→
List given quantities with units first. Convert mA→A, cm→m, min→s before substituting.
→
Decide series or parallel. Series → same current, add R. Parallel → same voltage, add reciprocals.
→
Reduce the network to one R before applying $V=IR$ to the whole circuit.
→
For heat/energy choose the right form: $H=I^2Rt$ when $I,R$ known; $P=\tfrac{V^2}{R}$ when $V,R$ known.
Pitfalls

Where students lose easy marks

✕
Adding resistances directly in parallel. You must add reciprocals — $R_p$ is always the smallest.
✕
Forgetting that current is the same in series but voltage is the same in parallel.
✕
Dropping the square on $I$ in $H=I^2Rt$.
✕
Mixing up kW (power) and kWh (energy) in cost problems.
✕
Not converting time to seconds when finding heat in joules.

The rigorous formal core

Derive the combination & heating laws

For Physics, the subject-adaptive third tab holds the chapter’s derivations — the formal apparatus CBSE can ask you to prove.

1 · Resistors in series

1
Same current $I$ flows through each resistor; the total voltage splits: $V=V_1+V_2+V_3$.
2
By Ohm’s law on each: $V_1=IR_1$, $V_2=IR_2$, $V_3=IR_3$, and on the whole: $V=IR_s$.
3
So $IR_s=IR_1+IR_2+IR_3$. Cancel $I$: $\boxed{R_s=R_1+R_2+R_3}$.

2 · Resistors in parallel

1
Same voltage $V$ across each; the total current splits: $I=I_1+I_2+I_3$.
2
$I_1=\tfrac{V}{R_1}$, $I_2=\tfrac{V}{R_2}$, $I_3=\tfrac{V}{R_3}$, and $I=\tfrac{V}{R_p}$.
3
So $\tfrac{V}{R_p}=\tfrac{V}{R_1}+\tfrac{V}{R_2}+\tfrac{V}{R_3}$. Cancel $V$: $\boxed{\dfrac{1}{R_p}=\dfrac{1}{R_1}+\dfrac{1}{R_2}+\dfrac{1}{R_3}}$.

3 · Joule’s law of heating

1
Work to move charge $Q$ through $V$ is $W=VQ$. With $Q=It$, energy supplied in time $t$ is $W=VIt$.
2
In a purely resistive circuit this energy is dissipated as heat: $H=VIt$.
3
Substitute Ohm’s law $V=IR$: $\boxed{H=I^2Rt}$ — Joule’s law of heating.
