Exemplar Q11 mark
A person cannot see distinctly objects kept beyond 2 m. This defect can be corrected by using a lens of power

Step-by-step solution

  1. The person has myopia with far point = 2 m (cannot see beyond 2 m).
  2. Correction requires a concave lens of focal length f = -2 m so it forms an image of a distant object at the far point.
  3. Power P = 1/f (in m) = 1/(-2) = -0.5 D.
  4. Hence the required lens has power -0.5 D (concave). Answer: (B)

Answer: – 0.5 D — option (b)

Exemplar Q21 mark
A student sitting on the last bench can read the letters written on the blackboard but is not able to read the letters written in his text book. Which of the following statements is correct?

Step-by-step solution

  1. The student can read the blackboard (distant object) clearly → far point is normal (= ∞).
  2. But he cannot read the textbook held in his hand (near object) → near point is farther than 25 cm.
  3. This is hypermetropia: near point has moved farther away from the eye.
  4. Hence option (a) is correct.

Answer: The near point of his eyes has receded away — option (a)

Exemplar Q61 mark
Twinkling of stars is due to atmospheric

Step-by-step solution

  1. The earth's atmosphere has layers of different refractive index due to temperature/density variation.
  2. Starlight is refracted continuously while passing through these layers.
  3. The apparent position and brightness fluctuate → star twinkles.
  4. Hence option (b) is correct.

Answer: refraction of light by different layers of varying refractive indices — option (b)

Exemplar Q71 mark
The clear sky appears blue because

Step-by-step solution

  1. Air molecules and fine particles in the atmosphere have sizes smaller than the wavelength of visible light.
  2. They scatter shorter wavelengths (violet/blue) more strongly than longer wavelengths (red).
  3. Scattered blue light enters our eyes from all directions → sky appears blue.
  4. Hence option (c) is correct.

Exemplar Q91 mark
The danger signals installed at the top of tall buildings are red in colour. These can be easily seen from a distance because among all other colours, the red light

Step-by-step solution

  1. Red light has the longest wavelength of all visible colours.
  2. Scattering by particles is more effective for shorter wavelengths and least for longer wavelengths.
  3. Red light is scattered the least → travels longer distances through fog/smoke without being deflected away.
  4. Hence red signals are visible from far away. Option (b) is correct.

Answer: is scattered the least by smoke or fog — option (b)

Exemplar Q173 marks
How are we able to see nearby and also the distant objects clearly?

Step-by-step solution

  1. The eye has the property of accommodation: ability to change the focal length of the eye lens.
  2. Ciliary muscles control the curvature of the lens.
  3. Distant objects: ciliary muscles relax → lens thin → focal length large → image on retina.
  4. Nearby objects: ciliary muscles contract → lens thick → focal length small → image on retina.
  5. Image distance stays constant (≈ 2.3 cm); only focal length changes → both near and distant objects appear clear.

Answer: Due to power of accommodation: ciliary muscles change the lens's curvature/focal length so the image of objects at any distance forms on the retina.

Exemplar Q213 marks
Is the position of a star as seen by us its true position? Justify your answer.

Step-by-step solution

  1. No, the apparent position is not the true position.
  2. Reason: atmospheric refraction. The atmosphere has layers of changing refractive index.
  3. Starlight bends continuously towards the normal as it enters denser layers → star appears higher than its real position.
  4. Atmospheric conditions are not stationary → the apparent position keeps fluctuating → twinkling.

Answer: No — atmospheric refraction shifts the apparent position (star appears higher) and makes it fluctuate.

Exemplar Q223 marks
Why do we see a rainbow in the sky only after rainfall?

Step-by-step solution

  1. A rainbow forms when sunlight passes through tiny water droplets in the atmosphere.
  2. Each droplet acts like a tiny prism: refraction + dispersion + internal reflection + refraction.
  3. After rainfall, atmosphere has many such droplets suspended in air.
  4. The Sun (behind observer) illuminates these droplets, and the dispersed colours reach the eye.
  5. No water droplets → no dispersion → no rainbow. Hence rainbow is seen only after rainfall.

Answer: Rainbow needs tiny water droplets in the atmosphere; these are present after rainfall, acting as prisms that disperse sunlight.

Exemplar Q233 marks
Why is the colour of the clear sky blue?

Step-by-step solution

  1. Atmospheric particles (air molecules) have sizes smaller than the wavelength of visible light.
  2. These particles scatter shorter wavelengths (blue) more than longer wavelengths (red).
  3. Red wavelength is about 1.8× that of blue → blue is scattered much more.
  4. Scattered blue light reaches our eyes from all directions → sky appears blue.
  5. If no atmosphere → no scattering → sky would appear dark (as for astronauts).

Answer: Air molecules scatter shorter (blue) wavelengths much more than longer (red) wavelengths; scattered blue light reaches our eyes from all directions, making the sky look blue.

Long answer questions

Exemplar Q305 marks
How does refraction take place in the atmosphere? Why do stars twinkle but not the planets?

Step-by-step solution

  1. Atmospheric refraction = refraction of light by layers of the earth's atmosphere with varying refractive index (temperature/density variation).
  2. Light bends continuously as it passes through these layers; physical conditions are not stationary → apparent position fluctuates.
  3. Stars are extremely far away → essentially POINT SOURCES of light.
  4. Atmospheric refraction makes their apparent position fluctuate and the amount of light reaching the eye fluctuates → stars TWINKLE.
  5. Planets are much closer → appear as EXTENDED sources (collection of many point sources). Twinkling of individual points AVERAGES OUT to zero across the disc → planets shine STEADILY (do not twinkle).

Answer: Atmospheric refraction = continuous bending by layers of changing μ. Stars (point sources) twinkle due to fluctuating refraction. Planets (extended sources) do not twinkle — fluctuations average out across the disc.
