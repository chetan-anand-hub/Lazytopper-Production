Chapter at a glance

Metals give up electrons - and that one habit explains all their chemistry.

Metals are lustrous, malleable, ductile conductors that tend to LOSE electrons and form positive ions, so they react with oxygen, water and acids and can push a less reactive metal out of its salt. Ordering metals by how readily they react gives the reactivity (activity) series, and that single list then tells us how each metal occurs in nature and how it must be extracted and refined. Non-metals behave in the opposite way - they gain electrons - so a metal and a non-metal together form an ionic compound. Most board marks here come from writing the correct balanced equation and reasoning with the activity series.

What the board asks

Distinguish metals from non-metals by their physical properties (lustre, malleability, ductility, conductivity, sonority) and their chemical behaviour; write and balance the equations for metals reacting with oxygen, water, dilute acids and salt solutions; use the reactivity (activity) series to predict displacement and to choose the right extraction method; explain how metals occur as minerals and ores and how they are enriched, reduced (by carbon or electrolysis) and refined; show how an ionic (electrovalent) compound forms by the transfer of electrons and list its properties; and explain corrosion, its prevention and alloys. The balanced equation and activity-series reasoning carry most of the marks here.

  • Physical properties of metals — 1-mark: name a metal that is liquid / can be cut with a knife / is the best conductor; or explain malleable and ductile.
  • Physical properties of non-metals — 2-mark: state exceptions (iodine lustrous, graphite conducts) and differentiate metals from non-metals.
  • Reaction of metals with oxygen — 3-mark: write equations for metals burning in oxygen; define amphoteric oxides with two examples.
  • Reaction of metals with water and acids — 3-mark: write equations for iron with steam and calcium/potassium with water; explain why sodium is stored in kerosene.
  • Reactivity series and displacement — 3-mark: tabulated reactivity comparison (metals A, B, C, D); predict displacement and arrange in decreasing reactivity.
  • Ionic (electrovalent) compounds and their properties — 3-mark: show the formation of Na2O or MgO by electron transfer; give the ions present; explain the high melting point.
  • Occurrence and extraction of metals — 3-mark: define mineral, ore, gangue; state the chemical process used to obtain a metal from its oxide.
  • Refining of metals — 3-mark: in the electrolytic refining of a metal M, state what is taken as anode, cathode and electrolyte.
  • Corrosion, its prevention and alloys — 2-mark: state two ways to prevent rusting; explain the conditions needed for iron to rust; what are alloys.

Key formulas

Metal + Oxygen
Metal + Oxygen -> Metal oxide (mostly basic; Al2O3 and ZnO are amphoteric)
Metal + Water
Metal + Water -> Metal oxide + Hydrogen . Metal oxide + Water -> Metal hydroxide
Metal + Acid
Metal + Dilute acid -> Salt + Hydrogen
Displacement
Metal A + Salt solution of B -> Salt solution of A + Metal B (A more reactive than B)
Activity series
K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Pb > [H] > Cu > Hg > Ag > Au
Roasting / Calcination
Roasting: sulphide ore + excess air -> oxide . Calcination: carbonate ore + limited air -> oxide
Thermit reaction
Fe2O3 + 2Al -> 2Fe + Al2O3 + Heat (aluminium reduces iron(III) oxide)

Definitions to know (NCERT wording)

  • Metallic lustre — Metals, in their pure state, have a shining surface. This property is called metallic lustre.
  • Malleability — Some metals can be beaten into thin sheets. This property is called malleability.
  • Ductility — The ability of metals to be drawn into thin wires is called ductility.
  • Amphoteric oxides — Such metal oxides which react with both acids as well as bases to produce salts and water are known as amphoteric oxides.
  • Reactivity (activity) series — The reactivity series is a list of metals arranged in the order of their decreasing activities.
  • Ionic (electrovalent) compounds — The compounds formed in this manner by the transfer of electrons from a metal to a non-metal are known as ionic compounds or electrovalent compounds.
  • Minerals — The elements or compounds, which occur naturally in the earth's crust, are known as minerals.
  • Ores — At some places, minerals contain a very high percentage of a particular metal and the metal can be profitably extracted from it. These minerals are called ores.
  • Metallurgy — The extraction of metals from their ores and then refining them for use is known as metallurgy.
  • Corrosion — The surface of some metals, such as iron, is corroded when they are exposed to moist air for a long period of time. This phenomenon is known as corrosion.
  • Alloy — An alloy is a homogeneous mixture of two or more metals, or a metal and a non-metal.
