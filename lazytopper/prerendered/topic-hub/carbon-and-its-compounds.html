Chapter at a glance

One element, millions of compounds - because carbon shares.

Carbon completes its octet not by giving or taking electrons but by sharing them, forming four strong covalent bonds. Two properties - catenation (carbon-to-carbon chains, branches and rings) and tetravalency - let carbon build an enormous family of compounds. Attach a functional group and a whole homologous series behaves alike, so learning one member teaches you the rest. The chapter's reactions - combustion, oxidation, addition, substitution, esterification and saponification - and the cleaning action of soap all follow from this covalent bonding.

What the board asks

Draw electron-dot (Lewis) structures for simple covalent molecules (H2, O2, N2, CH4, CO2 and compounds such as CH3Cl); give the two reasons - catenation and tetravalency - for carbon's huge number of compounds; define a homologous series and give an example; name a compound and identify its functional group; write balanced equations for combustion, the oxidation of ethanol to ethanoic acid, addition (hydrogenation), substitution, esterification and saponification, with the correct conditions above the arrow; differentiate ethanol and ethanoic acid by their physical and chemical properties; and explain the cleaning action of soap through micelle formation. Most marks sit in drawing the correct structure and writing the right reaction with its conditions.

  • Covalent bonding and electron-dot structures — 3-mark: draw the electron dot structure of a given molecule (e.g. CH3Cl, CO2, ethanoic acid) and explain the covalent bonding.
  • Allotropes of carbon — 2-mark: compare the structure and one physical property of diamond and graphite; name the C-60 allotrope.
  • Versatile nature of carbon (catenation + tetravalency) — 2-mark: state the two properties of carbon that lead to the huge number of carbon compounds.
  • Chains, branches, rings and isomers — 3-mark: draw the structures/structural isomers of a named compound (e.g. the isomers of pentane) and classify as saturated or unsaturated.
  • Homologous series, functional groups and naming — 3-mark: define a homologous series with an example; name compounds or identify the functional group from a formula.
  • Chemical properties of carbon compounds — identify the reaction type from an equation; name the products; state which hydrocarbons undergo addition reactions.
  • Ethanol, ethanoic acid, soaps and detergents — 3-mark: explain the cleaning action of soap via micelle formation; differentiate ethanol and ethanoic acid by their properties.

Definitions to know (NCERT wording)

  • Covalent bond — Such bonds which are formed by the sharing of an electron pair between two atoms are known as covalent bonds.
  • Catenation — Carbon has the unique ability to form bonds with other atoms of carbon, giving rise to large molecules. This property is called catenation.
  • Saturated compounds — Compounds of carbon, which are linked by only single bonds between the carbon atoms are called saturated compounds.
  • Unsaturated compounds — Compounds of carbon having double or triple bonds between their carbon atoms are called unsaturated compounds.
  • Hydrocarbons — All these carbon compounds which contain only carbon and hydrogen are called hydrocarbons.
  • Functional group — These heteroatoms and the group containing these confer specific properties to the compound, regardless of the length and nature of the carbon chain and hence are called functional groups.
  • Homologous series — Hence, such a series of compounds in which the same functional group substitutes for hydrogen in a carbon chain is called a homologous series.
