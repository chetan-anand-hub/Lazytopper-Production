Exemplar Q41 mark
A current of 1 A is drawn by a filament of an electric bulb. Number of electrons passing through a cross section of the filament in 16 seconds would be roughly

Step-by-step solution

  1. Total charge in 16 s: Q = I × t = 1 × 16 = 16 C.
  2. Number of electrons n = Q/e = 16 / (1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹) = 10 × 10¹⁹ = 10²⁰.
  3. Hence roughly 10²⁰ electrons pass through the cross-section. Answer: (A)

Answer: Option (a) — 10²⁰

Exemplar Q101 mark
A cylindrical conductor of length l and uniform area of cross-section A has resistance R. Another conductor of length 2l and resistance R of the same material has area of cross section
Step 1 R = ρl/A. For first wire: R = ρl/A.

Step-by-step solution

  1. R = ρl/A. For first wire: R = ρl/A.
  2. For second wire of length 2l and area A': ρ(2l)/A' = R.
  3. Setting equal: ρ(2l)/A' = ρl/A ⇒ A' = 2A.
  4. Hence the new area is 2A. Answer: (C)

Answer: Option (c) — 2A

Exemplar Q121 mark
If the current I through a resistor is increased by 100% (assume that temperature remains unchanged), the increase in power dissipated will be
Step 1 Power dissipated P = I²R; for constant R, P ∝ I².

Step-by-step solution

  1. Power dissipated P = I²R; for constant R, P ∝ I².
  2. Increasing I by 100% means new I' = 2I.
  3. New power P' = (2I)²R = 4I²R = 4P. Increase = P' − P = 3P.
  4. Percentage increase = (3P/P) × 100% = 300%. Answer: (C)

Answer: Option (c) — 300%

Exemplar Q161 mark
An electric kettle consumes 1 kW of electric power when operated at 220 V. A fuse wire of what rating must be used for it?

Step-by-step solution

  1. Current drawn: I = P/V = 1000 W / 220 V ≈ 4.55 A.
  2. The fuse rating must be just above the operating current.
  3. Among the given options, only 5 A is greater than 4.55 A.
  4. Hence a 5 A fuse must be used. Answer: (D)

Answer: Option (d) — 5 A

Short answer questions

Exemplar Q212 marks
Should the resistance of an ammeter be low or high? Give reason.

Step-by-step solution

  1. [0.5 mark] An ammeter is always connected in series, so its resistance adds to the total circuit resistance.
  2. [1 mark] If the ammeter resistance were high, total R would increase ⇒ current I = V/R would decrease.
  3. [0.5 mark] To measure the actual circuit current correctly, its resistance must be very low (ideally zero).

Answer: Very low — to avoid altering the current being measured.

Exemplar Q232 marks
How does use of a fuse wire protect electrical appliances?

Step-by-step solution

  1. [0.5 mark] A fuse is a piece of wire of suitable rating made of low-melting-point material, placed in series with the appliance.
  2. [0.5 mark] Under normal conditions current is below the rating and the fuse stays intact.
  3. [0.5 mark] If excessive current flows (overload/short-circuit), I²R heating melts the fuse wire and breaks the circuit.
  4. [0.5 mark] This disconnects the appliance from the supply before it is damaged or causes a fire.

Answer: Excess current melts the low-melting-point fuse wire, breaking the circuit and protecting the appliance.

Exemplar Q245 marks
What is electrical resistivity? In a series electrical circuit comprising a resistor made up of a metallic wire, the ammeter reads 5 A. The reading of the ammeter decreases to half when the length of the wire is doubled. Why?

Step-by-step solution

  1. [1 mark] Resistivity is an intrinsic property: ρ = RA/l. SI unit: ohm-metre (Ω m).
  2. [1 mark] It is the resistance of a sample of unit length and unit cross-section made of the material.
  3. [1 mark] Given R = ρl/A, when length is doubled (A, ρ unchanged), R' = ρ(2l)/A = 2R.
  4. [1 mark] By Ohm's law I = V/R; doubling R at constant V halves the current.
  5. [1 mark] New ammeter reading = 5/2 = 2.5 A.

Answer: Resistivity = RA/l (Ω m). Doubling length doubles R, so current halves to 2.5 A.

Exemplar Q252 marks
What is the commercial unit of electrical energy? Represent it in terms of joules.

Step-by-step solution

  1. [0.5 mark] Commercial unit of electrical energy = kilowatt-hour (kWh), also called 1 'unit'.
  2. [1 mark] 1 kWh = 1000 W × 1 h = 1000 W × 3600 s = 3.6 × 10⁶ W·s.
  3. [0.5 mark] Since 1 W·s = 1 J, 1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ J = 3,600,000 J.

Answer: 1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ J

Exemplar Q265 marks
A current of 1 ampere flows in a series circuit containing an electric lamp and a conductor of 5 Ω when connected to a 10 V battery. Calculate the resistance of the electric lamp. Now if a resistance of 10 Ω is connected in parallel with this series combination, what change (if any) in current flowing through 5 Ω conductor and potential difference across the lamp will take place? Give reason.
Step 1 [1 mark] Initial series circuit: Rtotal​ = V/I = 10 V / 1 A = 10 Ω.

Step-by-step solution

  1. [1 mark] Initial series circuit: Rtotal​ = V/I = 10 V / 1 A = 10 Ω.
  2. [1 mark] Resistance of lamp: R_L = Rtotal​ − 5 = 10 − 5 = 5 Ω.
  3. [0.5 mark] Now a 10 Ω resistor is connected in parallel with the series combination (lamp + 5 Ω = 10 Ω); the parallel combination is across the same 10 V battery.
  4. [0.5 mark] Potential difference across each branch of a parallel combination equals the battery voltage = 10 V.
  5. [1 mark] Series branch sees 10 V across 10 Ω → current = 1 A (unchanged); PD across lamp = 1 × 5 = 5 V (unchanged).
  6. [1 mark] Reason: in parallel, each branch is independent; adding a parallel branch does not change the voltage or current of an existing branch.

Answer: Rlamp​ = 5 Ω. No change — current through 5 Ω stays at 1 A; PD across lamp stays at 5 V.

Exemplar Q273 marks
Why is parallel arrangement used in domestic wiring?

Step-by-step solution

  1. [1 mark] In parallel each device gets the same supply voltage (e.g., 220 V) — devices run at their rated values.
  2. [0.5 mark] Each appliance draws the current it needs independently of others.
  3. [1 mark] If one appliance fails or is switched off, the others continue to work (the circuit is not broken).
  4. [0.5 mark] Different appliances of different ratings can be operated together; each is independently controlled by its own switch.

Answer: Each device runs at full mains voltage, is independent of others, and individually switchable.

Exemplar Q295 marks
Three incandescent bulbs of 100 W each are connected in series in an electric circuit. In another circuit another set of three bulbs of the same wattage are connected in parallel to the same source. (a) Will the bulb in the two circuits glow with the same brightness? Justify your answer. (b) Now let one bulb in both the circuits get fused. Will the rest of the bulbs continue to glow in each circuit? Give reason.

Step-by-step solution

  1. [0.5 mark] Each bulb has the same resistance R = V²/P at the rated voltage.
  2. [1.5 marks] (a) Series circuit: total resistance = 3R; current Is​ = V/(3R); power per bulb Ps​ = Is​² R = V²R/(9R²) = V²/(9R) = Prated​/9.
  3. [1 mark] Parallel circuit: each bulb has full voltage V across it; power per bulb = V²/R = Prated​.
  4. [0.5 mark] Hence each bulb in parallel glows much brighter (about 9 times) than in series.
  5. [1.5 marks] (b) Series: a single broken bulb breaks the only path → other bulbs go off. Parallel: each bulb on its own branch is independent → other bulbs continue to glow.

Answer: (a) Parallel bulbs are far brighter (Prated​ each) than series bulbs (Prated​/9 each). (b) Series: all stop. Parallel: others continue.

Exemplar Q315 marks
What is electrical resistivity of a material? What is its unit? Describe an experiment to study the factors on which the resistance of conducting wire depends.

Step-by-step solution

  1. [1 mark] Resistivity ρ = RA/l; SI unit Ω m. It is an intrinsic property of the material (depends on nature and temperature, not shape).
  2. [0.5 mark] Set-up: cell — plug key — ammeter — wire under test in series, voltmeter across the wire.
  3. [1 mark] Step 1 (length): replace the wire with one of the same material and area but double the length; the ammeter reading drops to half ⇒ R doubles ⇒ R ∝ l.
  4. [1 mark] Step 2 (area): replace the wire with a thicker one (larger A) of the same length and material; current rises ⇒ R falls ⇒ R ∝ 1/A.
  5. [1 mark] Step 3 (material): replace with a wire of different material (same l and A); current changes ⇒ R depends on the material (i.e., on ρ).
  6. [0.5 mark] Combining all three: R = ρl/A.

Answer: ρ = RA/l (unit Ω m). Experiment shows R ∝ l, R ∝ 1/A, and R depends on material.

Exemplar Q325 marks
How will you infer with the help of an experiment that the same current flows through every part of the circuit containing three resistances in series connected to a battery?

Step-by-step solution

  1. [1 mark] Apparatus: battery, plug key, three resistors R₁, R₂, R₃ joined end-to-end in series, ammeter and connecting wires.
  2. [1 mark] First, connect the ammeter in the main loop (say after the key) and note the reading I.
  3. [1 mark] Disconnect the ammeter; reconnect it between R₁ and R₂; note the reading.
  4. [1 mark] Repeat between R₂ and R₃, and after R₃ (before returning to the battery); note each reading.
  5. [1 mark] All four readings are equal — proving that the current is the same at every point in a series circuit.

Answer: Ammeter moved to different points in a series loop reads the same current — confirming I is same throughout.

Exemplar Q335 marks
How will you conclude that the same potential difference (voltage) exists across three resistors connected in a parallel arrangement to a battery?

Step-by-step solution

  1. [1 mark] Apparatus: battery, plug key, three resistors in parallel between two common points X and Y, and a voltmeter.
  2. [1 mark] Connect the voltmeter across the combination XY (across the battery terminals of the parallel block); note its reading V.
  3. [1 mark] Now connect the voltmeter across R₁ only — note the reading.
  4. [1 mark] Repeat across R₂ and across R₃ separately.
  5. [1 mark] All four readings are equal (= V), proving that in parallel each resistor has the same potential difference across it.

Answer: Voltmeter across each resistor reads the same value as across the combination — confirms equal PD in parallel.
