The Big Idea AUTHORED

Living bodies stay coordinated in two ways - fast electrical nerve signals and slower chemical hormones.

A multicellular body must recognise changes around it and respond with just the right action. Animals do this with a nervous system that carries information as electrical impulses and with hormones carried in the blood; plants, having no nerves or muscles, rely on changes in water content and on hormones that direct their growth. In Class 10 this chapter is examined mainly through labelled diagrams and step-by-step explanations, so most marks reward the exact NCERT terms and correctly labelled structures.

Definitions the board rewards NCERT VERBATIM

State these in NCERT’s exact words

Receptors
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 6,
“These receptors are usually located in our sense organs, such as the inner ear, the nose, the tongue, and so on.”
What it means: Specialised nerve-cell tips in the sense organs that detect a particular kind of stimulus - gustatory receptors detect taste, olfactory receptors detect smell.
Nervous tissue
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 6,
“Nervous tissue is made up of an organised network of nerve cells or neurons, and is specialised for conducting information via electrical impulses from one part of the body to another.”
What it means: The tissue built from neurons that carries information rapidly, as electrical impulses, from one part of the body to another.
Reflex arc
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 6,
“Such a connection is commonly called a reflex arc.”
What it means: The short nerve pathway - receptor to sensory neuron to spinal cord to motor neuron to effector - that gives a quick automatic response without waiting for the brain to think.
Central nervous system
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 6,
“The brain and spinal cord constitute the central nervous system.”
What it means: The brain plus the spinal cord - the main coordinating centre that receives information from all parts of the body and integrates it.
Cerebellum
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 6,
“These are possible due to a part of the hind-brain called the cerebellum. It is responsible for precision of voluntary actions and maintaining the posture and balance of the body.”
What it means: The part of the hind-brain that fine-tunes voluntary movements and keeps the body balanced - it lets you walk in a straight line, ride a bicycle or pick up a pencil.
Phototropism
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 6,
“So, in two different kinds of phototropic movement, shoots respond by bending towards light while roots respond by bending away from it.”
What it means: Directional growth of a plant part in response to light - the shoot grows towards it (positive), the root grows away from it (negative).
Auxin
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 6,
“When growing plants detect light, a hormone called auxin, synthesised at the shoot tip, helps the cells to grow longer.”
What it means: A plant hormone made at the shoot tip that makes cells elongate; by collecting on the shady side of the shoot it bends the shoot towards light.
Adrenaline
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 6,
“This is done in many animals, including human beings, using a hormone called adrenaline that is secreted from the adrenal glands.”
What it means: The fight-or-flight hormone from the adrenal glands - it makes the heart beat faster, raises the breathing rate and diverts blood to the skeletal muscles to ready the body for action.
📖Key terms — NCERT verbatim (state word-for-word)
Synapse
These chemicals cross the gap, or synapse, and start a similar electrical impulse in a dendrite of the next neuron. (Ch 6, )
Peripheral nervous system
The communication between the central nervous system and the other parts of the body is facilitated by the peripheral nervous system consisting of cranial nerves arising from the brain and spinal nerves arising from the spinal cord. (Ch 6, )
Medulla
All these involuntary actions including blood pressure, salivation and vomiting are controlled by the medulla in the hind-brain. (Ch 6, )
Geotropism
This upward and downward growth of shoots and roots, respectively, in response to the pull of earth or gravity is, obviously, geotropism. (Ch 6, )
Abscisic acid
Abscisic acid is one example of a hormone which inhibits growth. Its effects include wilting of leaves. (Ch 6, )
Thyroxin
Iodine is necessary for the thyroid gland to make thyroxin hormone. Thyroxin regulates carbohydrate, protein and fat metabolism in the body so as to provide the best balance for growth. (Ch 6, )
Insulin
This is a hormone which is produced by the pancreas and helps in regulating blood sugar levels. (Ch 6, )
Feedback mechanism
The timing and amount of hormone released are regulated by feedback mechanisms. (Ch 6, )
Must-know concepts AUTHORED FRAMING

…and where they’re tested

1
The neuron and the nerve impulse
Receptors in the sense organs pick up a stimulus at the dendritic tip of a neuron; this sets off an electrical impulse that travels dendrite to cell body to axon. At the axon end the impulse releases chemicals that cross the synapse to start a fresh impulse in the next neuron (or in a muscle or gland).
5-mark: draw and label a neuron and explain its function; what happens at the synapse between two neurons.
2
Reflex action and the reflex arc
A reflex is a quick, automatic response to a stimulus. Because conscious thinking would be too slow, the sensory nerve is connected to the motor nerve in the spinal cord, forming a reflex arc: receptor to sensory neuron to spinal cord (relay) to motor neuron to effector. The information still travels on to the brain, but the response does not wait for it.
5-mark: what is a reflex arc; trace a reflex action; how are reflex actions different from voluntary and involuntary actions.
3
The human brain and its protection
The brain has three regions - fore-brain (main thinking part; sensory areas, voluntary motor control, and a hunger centre), mid-brain and hind-brain. Involuntary actions such as blood pressure, salivation and vomiting are controlled by the medulla; posture, balance and precision of voluntary movement are controlled by the cerebellum. The brain sits in a bony box (cranium) cushioned by fluid, and the vertebral column protects the spinal cord.
5-mark: label the human brain and give the functions of cerebrum, cerebellum and medulla; how are the brain and spinal cord protected.
4
Coordination in plants - two kinds of movement
Plants have neither nerves nor muscles, yet they respond. Some movements are immediate and independent of growth - the sensitive plant (touch-me-not) folds its leaves by changing the amount of water in its cells, so they swell or shrink. Other movements are directional and caused by growth (tropisms), and are much slower.
3-mark: how is the movement of the sensitive plant different from the movement of a shoot towards light; how do plants respond without a nervous system.
5
Tropisms and plant hormones
Directional growth movements are named after the trigger: phototropism (light), geotropism (gravity), hydrotropism (water) and chemotropism (chemicals). Plant hormones coordinate this: auxin (made at the shoot tip) causes elongation and bending towards light, gibberellins help stem growth, cytokinins promote cell division (abundant in fruits and seeds), and abscisic acid inhibits growth and causes wilting.
3-mark: define phototropism and geotropism; name plant hormones and their functions; how does auxin make a shoot bend towards light.
6
Hormones and the endocrine system in animals
Animal hormones are secreted by endocrine glands directly into the blood and act on target organs. Adrenaline (adrenal glands) prepares the body for fight-or-flight; thyroxin (thyroid, needs iodine) regulates metabolism, and its deficiency causes goitre; insulin (pancreas) regulates blood sugar, and its deficiency causes diabetes; growth hormone (pituitary) controls growth, and its deficiency in childhood causes dwarfism; testosterone and oestrogen bring about the changes of puberty. A feedback mechanism keeps each hormone at the right level.
3-5 mark: match hormone-gland-function (Table 6.1); why is iodised salt advisable; how does the body respond to adrenaline; why are diabetics given insulin injections.

“[authored prompt] Trace, step by step, what happens in the reflex arc when you touch a hot object and pull your hand back.”
1
Receptor detects the stimulus.Heat and pain receptors in the skin of the hand detect the dangerous stimulus and generate a nerve impulse.
1 mark
2
Sensory neuron carries the input.The sensory neuron carries this impulse from the receptor towards the spinal cord.
1 mark
3
Relay in the spinal cord (the reflex arc).In the spinal cord the impulse is passed - through a relay neuron - straight from the incoming sensory nerve to the outgoing motor nerve, forming the reflex arc; the information also travels on to the brain.
1 mark
4
Motor neuron carries the output.The motor neuron carries the impulse from the spinal cord to the effector.
1 mark
5
Effector responds.The effector - the muscle in the arm - contracts and the hand is pulled back, all before the brain has consciously felt the pain.
1 mark
Mark-scheme logic (authored): CBSE 5-mark - 1 mark each for the receptor, the sensory neuron, the spinal-cord relay (the reflex arc), the motor neuron and the effector muscle response. The reflex arc is formed in the spinal cord so the response is quick; the signal still reaches the brain but the action does not wait for it.

Problem-solving strategies AUTHORED
→
Diagram question?Draw it large and label with straight ruled lines, using the exact NCERT terms - cerebellum not balance centre, medulla not brain stem, axon not tail. The neuron, reflex arc, human brain and endocrine-gland diagrams are the ones most often asked.
→
Differences between asked?Answer in a two-column table - reflex action vs walking (voluntary), reflex vs involuntary action, nervous vs hormonal control, sensitive-plant movement vs growth movement. One crisp point per row scores faster than a paragraph.
→
Hormone question?Pair every hormone with its gland AND its function (Table 6.1): adrenaline-adrenal gland-fight or flight, thyroxin-thyroid-metabolism (needs iodine), insulin-pancreas-blood sugar, growth hormone-pituitary-growth. Examiners credit the gland and the function separately.
→
Why or how add-on?Board answers want the reason, not just the description - a reflex arc is quicker because the response is decided in the spinal cord, not the brain; iodised salt is advised because iodine is needed to make thyroxin. State the cause.
Common mistakes that cost marks

Where students lose easy marks

✕
Writing that a reflex action is controlled by the brain - it is completed by the spinal-cord reflex arc; the impulse also reaches the brain, but the response does not wait for it.
✕
Swapping the neuron parts: the dendrite RECEIVES information, the axon CARRIES the impulse away, and the gap between two neurons is the synapse (not the axon).
✕
Mixing up cerebellum and medulla: the cerebellum controls posture, balance and precision of voluntary movement; the medulla controls involuntary actions such as blood pressure, salivation and vomiting.
✕
Confusing plant hormones: auxin, gibberellin and cytokinin PROMOTE growth, while abscisic acid INHIBITS growth (it causes wilting). Naming abscisic acid as a growth promoter loses the mark.
✕
Calling insulin or adrenaline a plant hormone - the plant hormones are auxin, gibberellin, cytokinin and abscisic acid; insulin, adrenaline, thyroxin and growth hormone are animal hormones.

3 competency questions from the CBSE board pattern, with the full step-marked solution for each — the marks are shown per step, as the official scheme awards them.

5 marksSection D

(a) How does blood sugar level get regulated in the human body? (b) (i) Which hormone is secreted into the blood when you are under stress? Name the gland that secretes this hormone. (ii) How does it help the body to cope up in an emergency situation?

Step-marked solution
  1. [1 mark] (a) Regulation of blood sugar: When the blood sugar level rises, the beta-cells of the pancreas detect it and secrete more of the hormone INSULIN.
  2. [1 mark] (a) Insulin lowers the blood sugar level by promoting the uptake of glucose and its conversion into glycogen; when the level falls, less insulin is secreted — this is controlled by a FEEDBACK MECHANISM.
  3. [1 mark] (b)(i) Under stress the hormone ADRENALINE is secreted into the blood; it is secreted by the ADRENAL GLAND, situated above the kidneys.
  4. [1 mark] (b)(ii) Adrenaline acts on the heart, making it beat faster so that more oxygenated blood is supplied to the skeletal muscles.
  5. [1 mark] (b)(ii) It also increases the breathing rate through contraction of the diaphragm and rib muscles and diverts blood to the muscles — preparing the body for a 'fight or flight' response.
4 marksSection E

With reference to Fig. 1.1, explain how the plant hormone controls the growth response of the shoot.

Step-marked solution
  1. [1 mark] The hormone (auxin) is produced/made at the shoot tip.
  2. [1 mark] The hormone moves / diffuses / passes / spreads down the shoot.
  3. [1 mark] It collects on the side away from the light (the shaded side).
  4. [1 mark] The hormone causes cell elongation on the shaded side (e.g. by stretching of cell walls / osmosis of water), so the shoot bends towards the light.
1 markSection A

Sapna suffers from a condition due to which her average blood sugar level is 174 mg/dL. The average blood sugar level in a healthy adult is <140 mg/dL. Which of the following could be the cause of Sapna's condition?

Step-marked solution
  1. [1 mark] Correct option: (2) insufficient production of insulin in her body. Insulin lowers blood glucose; too little of it leaves the blood sugar persistently high, which is what her reading shows. Excess insulin would lower it, and thyroxine regulates metabolism rather than blood glucose directly.
