Electricity

★

What the board actually asks: resistance combinations (series & parallel), Ohm's-law and R=ρl/A numericals with correct units, electric power (P=VI=I2R=V2/R) and energy in kW h, and Joule's-law heating — the numericals here carry almost every mark.

NCERT VERBATIM sourced, word-for-wordAUTHORED LazyTopper teaching scaffolding
The Big Idea AUTHORED

A potential difference pushes charge; resistance decides how much flows — and the leftover energy turns to heat.

One relation, V=IR, ties together current, potential difference and resistance. Combine resistors (series adds, parallel reciprocals add), then track the energy: P=VI and H=I2Rt. Almost every board mark here is a numerical, so signs, units and the right formula choice are what score.

Definitions the board rewards NCERT VERBATIM

State these in NCERT’s exact words

Electric current
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 11,
“A stream of electrons moving through a conductor constitutes an electric current. Conventionally, the direction of current is taken opposite to the direction of flow of electrons.”
What it means: Charge in motion. Quantitatively I=tQ​ (charge per unit time); SI unit ampere (A). Conventional current runs from + to − — opposite to the electrons.
Electric potential difference
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 11,
“We define the electric potential difference between two points in an electric circuit carrying some current as the work done to move a unit charge from one point to the other”
What it means: V=QW​ — the work done per unit charge moved between two points. SI unit volt (V), where 1V=1J C−1.
Ohm's law
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 11,
“The potential difference, V, across the ends of a given metallic wire in an electric circuit is directly proportional to the current flowing through it, provided its temperature remains the same. This is called Ohm's law.”
What it means: V∝I, so V=IR with R constant at fixed temperature. On a V–I graph this is a straight line through the origin whose slope is R.
Resistance
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 11,
“R is a constant for the given metallic wire at a given temperature and is called its resistance. It is the property of a conductor to resist the flow of charges through it.”
What it means: How strongly a conductor opposes current: R=IV​. SI unit ohm (Ω). Double R and (at fixed V) the current halves.
Electrical resistivity
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 11,
“where ρ (rho) is a constant of proportionality and is called the electrical resistivity of the material of the conductor. The SI unit of resistivity is Ω m. It is a characteristic property of the material.”
What it means: The material's own opposition, independent of shape: R=ρAl​. Metals/alloys ∼10−8–10−6Ωm; insulators far higher.
Heating effect of electric current
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 11,
“if the electric circuit is purely resistive, that is, a configuration of resistors only connected to a battery; the source energy continually gets dissipated entirely in the form of heat. This is known as the heating effect of electric current.”
What it means: In a resistor-only circuit all the supplied energy becomes heat: H=VIt. This is what electric heaters, irons, toasters and bulb filaments use.
Joule's law of heating
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 11,
“This is known as Joule's law of heating. The law implies that heat produced in a resistor is (i) directly proportional to the square of current for a given resistance, (ii) directly proportional to resistance for a given current, and (iii) directly proportional to the time for which the current flows through the resistor.”
What it means: H=I2Rt. Heat rises with the square of current, linearly with resistance, and linearly with time.
Electric power
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 11,
“Equation (11.21) gives the rate at which electric energy is dissipated or consumed in an electric circuit. This is also termed as electric power.”
What it means: The rate of using electrical energy: P=VI=I2R=RV2​. SI unit watt (W); the commercial unit of energy is the kilowatt hour (kW h).
📖Key terms — NCERT verbatim (state word-for-word)
Ampere
One ampere is constituted by the flow of one coulomb of charge per second (Ch 11, )
Volt
One volt is the potential difference between two points in a current carrying conductor when 1 joule of work is done to move a charge of 1 coulomb from one point to the other. (Ch 11, )
Ohm (unit)
If the potential difference across the two ends of a conductor is 1 V and the current through it is 1 A, then the resistance R, of the conductor is 1 Ω. (Ch 11, )
Rheostat
In an electric circuit, a device called rheostat is often used to change the resistance in the circuit. (Ch 11, )
Equivalent resistance in series
The equivalent resistance of several resistors in series is equal to the sum of their individual resistances. (Ch 11, )
Equivalent resistance in parallel
the reciprocal of the equivalent resistance of a group of resistances joined in parallel is equal to the sum of the reciprocals of the individual resistances. (Ch 11, )

…and where they’re tested

1
Electric current, charge & the circuit
Charge flows only around a continuous, closed path (an electric circuit). Current is the rate of that flow, I=tQ​, measured by an ammeter placed in series. The schematic below fixes the standard symbols and the +→− direction of conventional current.
1-mark define-the-unit / direction-of-current
2
Ohm's law & resistance
V=IR: at constant temperature the potential difference is proportional to the current, so a V–I plot is a straight line through the origin with slope R. A voltmeter (in parallel) reads V; an ammeter (in series) reads I.
2–3 mark Ohm's-law numerical / V–I graph
3
What resistance depends on — resistivity
R=ρAl​: resistance grows with length, falls with cross-sectional area, and depends on the material through its resistivity ρ. Alloys have higher ρ (and don't oxidise), so heating elements use them.
3-mark R=ρl/A numerical (watch the A=πr2)
4
Combining resistors — series vs parallel
Series (same current everywhere): Rs​=R1​+R2​+R3​, larger than any one resistor. Parallel (same voltage across each): Rp​1​=R1​1​+R2​1​+R3​1​, smaller than the smallest. Household wiring is parallel so each device gets full voltage and works independently.
3–5 mark combination numerical + derivation
5
Power, Joule heating & energy billed
Power is the rate of energy use: P=VI=I2R=RV2​ (watt). Heat dissipated in a resistor follows Joule's law H=I2Rt. Energy sold commercially is in kilowatt hours, where 1kW h=3.6×106J.
3–5 mark power/heating numerical + cost in kW h

Problem-solving strategies AUTHORED
→
Numerical?List every quantity with its SI unit before substituting — convert minutes to seconds, kW to W, mm diameter to m. Most lost marks are unit/conversion slips, not algebra.
→
Series or parallel?Series: same current, resistances add (Rs​ bigger than the largest). Parallel: same voltage, reciprocals add (Rp​ smaller than the smallest). Check your answer against those two bounds.
→
Which power formula?Pick the form matching the data: P=VI (given V and I), P=I2R (given I and R), P=V2/R (given V and R). All three are the same equation.
→
Energy or cost question?Work in kilowatt hours: energy (kW h) = power (kW) × time (h); cost = units × rate. Remember 1kW h=3.6×106J.
→
Resistivity numerical?In R=ρl/A the area is A=πr2 — halve the diameter to get the radius first, or you will be off by a factor of four.
Common mistakes that cost marks

Where students lose easy marks

✕
Adding parallel resistances directly (R1​+R2​) instead of adding reciprocals — the #1 combination error.
✕
Dropping units on the final answer (A, V, Ω, J, W) — a numerical answer without its unit loses a mark.
✕
Using the diameter as the radius in A=πr2 when applying R=ρl/A — gives an area four times too big.
✕
Not converting time or power units — minutes must become seconds, and kilowatts must become watts, before substituting.
✕
Confusing where meters go — the ammeter is in series and the voltmeter is in parallel, not the reverse.

The core skills here are wiring the meters correctly, reading circuit symbols, and knowing exactly how current, voltage and resistance behave in series vs parallel. These rules make every numerical and circuit-diagram question fall out.

Meters & current direction

  • AAmmeter measures current — always connected in series.
  • VVoltmeter measures potential difference — always connected in parallel across the two points.
  • →Conventional current flows from the + terminal to the − terminal (opposite to electron flow).

Series combination

  • ISame current through every resistor.
  • VPotential differences add: V=V1​+V2​+V3​.
  • RResistances add: Rs​=R1​+R2​+R3​ — bigger than the largest resistor.

Parallel combination

  • VSame potential difference across every resistor.
  • ICurrents add: I=I1​+I2​+I3​.
  • RReciprocals add: Rp​1​=R1​1​+R2​1​+R3​1​ — smaller than the smallest resistor.
Reading a circuit diagram (Table 11.1 symbols)
1
Cell: long thin line is the + terminal, short thick line is the − terminal; several cells in a row make a battery.
2
Plug key / switch: open (○) means no current; closed (⊙) completes the circuit.
3
A resistor is a zig-zag (or a plain rectangle); a rheostat is a resistor with a sliding arrow for variable resistance.
4
A dot at a wire crossing means the wires are joined; no dot means they cross without connecting.
