Coordinate Geometry

★

What the board actually asks: the Distance Formula (length of a side, type of triangle or quadrilateral, collinearity, equidistant points) and the Section Formula for internal division — including the mid-point and the points of trisection. In the rationalised 2026-27 chapter these two formulas carry essentially every mark; area-of-a-triangle by coordinates has been removed from the syllabus.

The Big Idea AUTHORED

Geometry you can compute.

Coordinate geometry turns points into number-pairs so that lengths and dividing-points become arithmetic. Two tools do all the work: the Distance Formula gives the length between any two points P(x1​,y1​) and Q(x2​,y2​), and the Section Formula gives the point that splits the segment AB internally in a chosen ratio m1​:m2​ (with the mid-point as the special 1:1 case). Everything else here — proving a triangle's type, testing whether three points are collinear, finding a missing vertex — is one of these two formulas applied.

Definitions the board rewards NCERT VERBATIM

State these in NCERT’s exact words

Distance formula
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 7,
“So, the distance between the points P(x₁, y₁) and Q(x₂, y₂) is PQ = √((x₂ – x₁)² + (y₂ – y₁)²), which is called the distance formula.”
What it means: The straight-line distance between two points: square the gap in x, square the gap in y, add them, take the positive square root — PQ=(x2​−x1​)2+(y2​−y1​)2​. The order of subtraction doesn't matter because each difference is squared.
Section formula (internal)
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 7,
“So, the coordinates of the point P(x, y) which divides the line segment joining the points A(x₁, y₁) and B(x₂, y₂), internally, in the ratio m₁ : m₂ are ((m₁x₂ + m₂x₁)/(m₁ + m₂), (m₁y₂ + m₂y₁)/(m₁ + m₂)). This is known as the section formula.”
What it means: Where a point lands when it splits the segment from A to B internally in the ratio m1​:m2​ — a weighted average of the endpoints. Watch the cross-pattern: the weight m1​ (the PA part) multiplies x2​, the coordinate of B.
Mid-point formula
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 7,
“Special Case : The mid-point of a line segment divides the line segment in the ratio 1 : 1. Therefore, the coordinates of the mid-point P of the join of the points A(x₁, y₁) and B(x₂, y₂) is ((x₁ + x₂)/2, (y₁ + y₂)/2).”
What it means: Just average the two x's and the two y's — (2x1​+x2​​,2y1​+y2​​). It is the section formula at ratio 1:1.
📖Key terms — NCERT verbatim (state word-for-word)
Abscissa (x-coordinate)
The distance of a point from the y-axis is called its x-coordinate, or abscissa. (Ch 7, )
Ordinate (y-coordinate)
The distance of a point from the x-axis is called its y-coordinate, or ordinate. (Ch 7, )
Distance from the origin
In particular, the distance of a point P(x, y) from the origin O(0, 0) is given by OP = √(x² + y²). (Ch 7, )
Section formula in k : 1 form
If the ratio in which P divides AB is k : 1, then the coordinates of the point P will be ((kx₂ + x₁)/(k + 1), (ky₂ + y₁)/(k + 1)). (Ch 7, )
Must-know concepts AUTHORED FRAMING

…and where they’re tested

1
Distance between two points
PQ=(x2​−x1​)2+(y2​−y1​)2​ — the length of the segment joining P(x1​,y1​) and Q(x2​,y2​). The distance from the origin is the special case OP=x2+y2​. Read the two differences straight off the coordinates, then square, add, and take the positive root.
2–3 mark “find the distance / length of a side”
2
The distance formula in disguise — triangles, squares, collinearity
Most questions never say “distance”. To name a triangle, compare its three side-lengths (and check a Pythagoras relation for a right angle); to prove a square, show four equal sides and equal diagonals; to test collinearity, check that the two shorter distances add to the longest (AB+BC=AC). All of it is the distance formula, applied a few times.
prove the type of a triangle / quadrilateral, or show three points are collinear (2–3 marks)
3
Section formula — internal division
The point P that splits AB internally in the ratio m1​:m2​ is (m1​+m2​m1​x2​+m2​x1​​,m1​+m2​m1​y2​+m2​y1​​). The weights cross over — m1​ multiplies the coordinates of B. In the rationalised syllabus only internal division is examined; external division is left for higher classes.
find the dividing point, or the ratio — including case-study setups (2–3 marks)
4
Mid-point & points of trisection
The mid-point is just the average of the coordinates: (2x1​+x2​​,2y1​+y2​​). The two points of trisection split AB into three equal parts — apply the section formula at ratios 1:2 and 2:1. A common exam use: a missing vertex of a parallelogram, since its diagonals bisect each other (equal mid-points).
mid-point, points of trisection, or the missing vertex of a parallelogram
5
Working backwards — find a ratio or a coordinate
When the ratio is unknown, assume it is k:1, apply the section formula, and solve the coordinate you already know. A point on the y-axis has x=0; a point on the x-axis has y=0 — that single fact becomes the equation you solve. Equidistant conditions (AP=BP) square to a neat linear relation between x and y.
“in what ratio does the y-axis divide…”, find k, or an equidistant-point relation

→
Need a length or the type of a shape?Distance formula on every relevant pair, then compare. Equal sides, equal diagonals, or a Pythagoras relation names the figure.
→
Three points — collinear?Compute the three distances; if the two smaller ones sum to the largest, the points are collinear — no area needed.
→
Split a segment in a ratio?Section formula. Keep the weights straight: m1​ pairs with the FAR point (the cross pattern).
→
Mid-point or trisection?Mid-point = average the coordinates. Trisection = section formula at 1:2 and again at 2:1.
→
“In what ratio…” or “find k”?Assume the ratio is k:1, apply the section formula, and use the coordinate you know — 0 on an axis — as the equation to solve.
Common mistakes that cost marks

Where students lose easy marks

✕
Swapping the section-formula weights — writing m1​x1​ instead of m1​x2​. The ratio's first part multiplies the SECOND point's coordinate.
✕
Sign slips with negative coordinates — (x2​−x1​) with x1​ negative becomes a sum, e.g. 3−(−2)=5, not 1.
✕
Dropping the square root, or taking the negative root — distance is always the positive square root.
✕
Forgetting the axis condition — a point on the y-axis has x=0 (on the x-axis, y=0); that is the equation you solve for the ratio or the point.

The Distance Formula — straight from Pythagoras

NCERT builds the distance formula on p.102 by dropping perpendiculars to the x-axis and reading off a right triangle (Fig. 7.5). The two legs come directly from the coordinates, and one application of the Pythagoras theorem gives the length — this is exactly why the formula has that squared-differences shape.

1
Set up the two points and drop perpendiculars.
P(x1​,y1​),Q(x2​,y2​)
Draw PR and QS perpendicular to the x-axis; a perpendicular from P meets QS at T (NCERT Fig. 7.5).
2
Read the horizontal leg from the x-coordinates.
PT=RS=x2​−x1​
3
Read the vertical leg from the y-coordinates.
QT=y2​−y1​
4
Apply the Pythagoras theorem in right triangle PTQ.
PQ2=PT2+QT2=(x2​−x1​)2+(y2​−y1​)2
5
Take the positive square root — distance is non-negative.
PQ=(x2​−x1​)2+(y2​−y1​)2​
This is the distance formula.

3 competency questions from the CBSE board pattern, with the full step-marked solution for each — the marks are shown per step, as the official scheme awards them.

5 marksSection D

Three consecutive vertices of a parallelogram ABCD taken in order are A(1, 2), B(4, 3) and C(6, 6). Using the property that the diagonals of a parallelogram bisect each other, find the coordinates of the fourth vertex D.

Step-marked solution
  1. [1 mark] Property: diagonals of a parallelogram bisect each other ⇒ midpoint of diagonal AC = midpoint of diagonal BD.
  2. [1 mark] Midpoint of AC = ((1+6)/2, (2+6)/2) = (7/2, 4).
  3. [1 mark] Let D = (x, y). Midpoint of BD = ((4+x)/2, (3+y)/2).
  4. [1 mark] Equate x: (4+x)/2 = 7/2 ⇒ x = 3. Equate y: (3+y)/2 = 4 ⇒ y = 5.
  5. [1 mark] D = (3, 5). Verify: B−A = (3, 1) and C−D = (3, 1) so AB ∥ = DC. Hence ABCD is a parallelogram.
5 marksSection D

Verify that the points A(−2, −1), B(1, 0), C(4, 3) and D(1, 2), taken in order, form a parallelogram. Using the lengths of the diagonals, decide further whether it is a rectangle.

Step-marked solution
  1. [1 mark] AB = √[(1+2)²+(0+1)²] = √10 and CD = √[(1−4)²+(2−3)²] = √10.
  2. [1 mark] BC = √[(4−1)²+(3−0)²] = √18 and DA = √[(−2−1)²+(−1−2)²] = √18.
  3. [1 mark] AB = CD and BC = DA ⇒ both pairs of opposite sides equal ⇒ ABCD is a parallelogram.
  4. [1 mark] Diagonal AC = √[(4+2)²+(3+1)²] = √52; diagonal BD = √[(1−1)²+(2−0)²] = 2.
  5. [1 mark] AC ≠ BD, so the diagonals are unequal ⇒ ABCD is a parallelogram but NOT a rectangle.
5 marksSection D

The corners of a rhombus-shaped tile are marked A(3, 0), B(4, 5), C(−1, 4) and D(−2, −1). Show that all four sides are equal, and by comparing the diagonals prove that the tile is a rhombus but not a square.

Step-marked solution
  1. [1 mark] AB = √[(4−3)²+(5−0)²] = √26 and BC = √[(−1−4)²+(4−5)²] = √26.
  2. [1 mark] CD = √[(−2+1)²+(−1−4)²] = √26 and DA = √[(3+2)²+(0+1)²] = √26.
  3. [1 mark] AB = BC = CD = DA = √26 ⇒ all four sides equal ⇒ ABCD is a rhombus.
  4. [1 mark] Diagonal AC = √[(−1−3)²+(4−0)²] = √32; diagonal BD = √[(−2−4)²+(−1−5)²] = √72.
  5. [1 mark] AC ≠ BD ⇒ the diagonals are unequal, so it is a rhombus but NOT a square.
