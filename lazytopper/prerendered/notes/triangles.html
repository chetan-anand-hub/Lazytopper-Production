The Big Idea AUTHORED

Same shape, any size.

Two figures are similar when they have the same shape but not necessarily the same size - one is a scaled copy of the other. For triangles this means equal corresponding angles and proportional corresponding sides. The chapter gives you shortcuts (the AAA, SSS and SAS criteria) to decide similarity from just three facts, the Basic Proportionality Theorem to split sides in ratio, and a way to measure the unmeasurable - heights of towers and lengths of shadows - by comparing similar triangles.

Definitions the board rewards NCERT VERBATIM

State these in NCERT’s exact words

Similar figures
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 6,
“Two figures having the same shape but not necessarily the same size are called similar figures.”
What it means: same shape, any size. Every pair of congruent figures is automatically similar, but similar figures need not be congruent (a small photo and its enlargement are similar, not congruent).
Similar polygons
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 6,
“Two polygons of the same number of sides are similar, if (i) their corresponding angles are equal and (ii) their corresponding sides are in the same ratio (or proportion).”
What it means: for polygons you need BOTH conditions: every corresponding angle equal AND every corresponding side in the same ratio. Either one alone is not enough (a square and a rectangle share angles but not ratios; a square and a rhombus share ratios but not angles).
Similar triangles
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 6,
“Two triangles are similar, if (i) their corresponding angles are equal and (ii) their corresponding sides are in the same ratio (or proportion).”
What it means: the same two conditions as polygons - but for triangles, verifying ONE of them automatically forces the other. That is exactly what the AAA/SSS/SAS criteria below exploit, so you never have to check all six pairs.
Basic Proportionality Theorem (Theorem 6.1)
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 6,
“Theorem 6.1 : If a line is drawn parallel to one side of a triangle to intersect the other two sides in distinct points, the other two sides are divided in the same ratio.”
What it means: the Thales Theorem: a line parallel to one side of a triangle cuts the other two sides proportionally, DBAD​=ECAE​. This is the ONE theorem in this chapter CBSE can ask you to PROVE - see the Proof tab.
AAA (Angle-Angle-Angle) similarity criterion (Theorem 6.3)
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 6,
“Theorem 6.3 : If in two triangles, corresponding angles are equal, then their corresponding sides are in the same ratio (or proportion) and hence the two triangles are similar.”
What it means: equal angles ⇒ proportional sides ⇒ similar. Since a triangle's third angle is fixed once two are known, two equal pairs of angles are already enough - that shorter form is the AA criterion.
SAS (Side-Angle-Side) similarity criterion (Theorem 6.5)
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 6,
“Theorem 6.5 : If one angle of a triangle is equal to one angle of the other triangle and the sides including these angles are proportional, then the two triangles are similar.”
What it means: one equal angle that sits BETWEEN two pairs of sides in the same ratio ⇒ similar. The equal angle must be the included angle - the one between the two proportional sides.
📖Key terms — NCERT verbatim (state word-for-word)
Scale factor
Note that the same ratio of the corresponding sides is referred to as the scale factor (or the Representative Fraction) for the polygons. (Ch 6, )
Converse of BPT (Theorem 6.2)
Theorem 6.2 : If a line divides any two sides of a triangle in the same ratio, then the line is parallel to the third side. (Ch 6, )
SSS (Side-Side-Side) similarity criterion (Theorem 6.4)
If in two triangles, corresponding sides are in the same ratio, then their corresponding angles are equal and hence the triangles are similar. (Ch 6, )
AA similarity criterion
If two angles of one triangle are respectively equal to two angles of another triangle, then the two triangles are similar. (Ch 6, )
Must-know concepts AUTHORED FRAMING

…and where they’re tested

1
Similar vs congruent - same shape, any size
Congruent figures match in shape AND size; similar figures match in shape only. So all congruent figures are similar, but the converse is not true. All circles are similar, all squares are similar, all equilateral triangles are similar - because their shape is fixed.
1-mark fill-in-the-blank / MCQ (Exercise 6.1 style)
2
The two similarity conditions (and why triangles are special)
For any pair of polygons, similarity needs BOTH equal corresponding angles AND proportional corresponding sides. For triangles only, one condition automatically brings the other - which is why the three criteria each need just three facts. When you write the similarity, match vertices in order: △ABC∼△DEF means ∠A=∠D, ∠B=∠E, ∠C=∠F.
State the criterion + write the similarity in correct vertex order (Exercise 6.3 Q1)
3
Basic Proportionality Theorem (Thales) and its converse
BPT (Theorem 6.1): a line parallel to one side divides the other two sides in the same ratio, DBAD​=ECAE​. Its converse (Theorem 6.2) runs backwards: if a line divides two sides in the same ratio, it must be parallel to the third side. Use BPT to find a missing length; use the converse to prove two lines parallel.
2-3 mark find EC/AD when DE || BC; prove a line is parallel (Exercise 6.2)
4
Picking a criterion - AAA/AA, SSS, SAS
AA: two pairs of equal angles. SSS: all three pairs of sides in the same ratio. SAS: one pair of equal angles with the two including sides in the same ratio. Scan the figure for what you are given - equal angles (from parallels, vertically opposite, or common angles) point to AA; three known side-ratios point to SSS.
State which pairs of triangles are similar and name the criterion (Exercise 6.3 Q1)
5
Measuring the unmeasurable - heights, shadows, distances
Similar triangles let you find a height or distance you cannot reach: set up two triangles that share equal angles (usually AA - a common angle plus two right angles), then equate a pair of corresponding sides and solve. This is the principle behind finding heights of towers, lengths of shadows, and even distances of far-off objects.
3-4 mark height-of-tower / length-of-shadow word problem (Example 7, Exercise 6.3 Q15)

Problem-solving strategies AUTHORED
→
Two triangles - are they similar?Hunt for equal angles first (from parallels, vertically opposite, or a common angle) -> AA. No angles? Check if all three side pairs share a ratio -> SSS. One angle plus its two including sides in ratio -> SAS.
→
Need to PROVE two lines parallel?Show the line divides two sides of the triangle in the same ratio, then quote Theorem 6.2 (converse of BPT).
→
A line is drawn parallel to a side?Quote Theorem 6.1 (BPT) at once: it splits the other two sides in the same ratio.
→
Height / shadow / distance you cannot measure?Build two similar triangles (usually AA - a common angle and two right angles), then equate a pair of corresponding sides and solve.
→
Writing the similarity statement?Match vertices in order. ∠A=∠D forces △ABC∼△DEF - never △ABC∼△EDF. The ratio chain follows the same order.
Common mistakes that cost marks

Where students lose easy marks

✕
Wrong vertex order in the similarity - writing △ABC∼△EDF instead of △ABC∼△DEF breaks every ratio and angle that follows.
✕
Assuming one condition is enough for polygons - a square and a rectangle have equal angles but not equal ratios; a square and a rhombus have equal ratios but not equal angles. Only for TRIANGLES does one condition imply the other.
✕
Confusing BPT with its converse - Theorem 6.1 needs the line GIVEN parallel and concludes a ratio; Theorem 6.2 needs the ratio GIVEN equal and concludes parallel. Quote the right direction.
✕
Pairing the wrong sides in the ratio - after △ABC∼△DEF, the ratio is DEAB​=EFBC​=FDCA​, not EFAB​. Follow the vertex correspondence.
✕
In SAS, using a non-included angle - the equal angle must sit BETWEEN the two proportional sides, or SAS does not apply.

Basic Proportionality Theorem (Theorem 6.1) - the area proof

In the rationalised 2026-27 syllabus, BPT (Theorem 6.1) is the one theorem in this chapter marked '(Prove)' - its formal proof is examinable. The three criteria (AAA/SSS/SAS) and the converse are '(Motivate)' - you must know the statements but are not asked for their formal proofs. Below is NCERT's area-ratio proof of BPT.

1
Given: △ABC with a line DE∥BC meeting AB at D and AC at E. To prove that the two sides are divided in the same ratio.
DBAD​=ECAE​
2
Join BE and CD, and drop perpendiculars DM⊥AC and EN⊥AB to serve as the heights.
3
Write the areas of the two triangles on base AD and DB (common height EN).
ar(ADE)=21​AD×EN,ar(BDE)=21​DB×EN
4
Likewise for the two triangles on base AE and EC (common height DM).
ar(ADE)=21​AE×DM,ar(DEC)=21​EC×DM
5
Divide each pair - the common heights cancel, leaving the side ratios.
ar(BDE)ar(ADE)​=DBAD​(1),ar(DEC)ar(ADE)​=ECAE​(2)
6
△BDE and △DEC sit on the same base DE and between the same parallels BC and DE, so they have equal area.
ar(BDE)=ar(DEC)(3)
Triangles on the same base and between the same parallels are equal in area (Class IX result).
7
From (1), (2) and (3): the numerators are the same ar(ADE) and the denominators are equal, so the two ratios are equal.
DBAD​=ECAE​
This proves BPT; the converse (Theorem 6.2) is obtained by running the argument backwards.
