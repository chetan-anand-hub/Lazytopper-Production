What the board actually asks: the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic and its two workhorses: HCF & LCM by prime factorisation (with HCF × LCM = product of the two numbers), and proving numbers like √2, √3, √5 irrational by contradiction. In the rationalised 2026-27 syllabus this chapter is short and mark-dense — prime-factorisation HCF/LCM and one irrationality proof carry almost every mark.

The Big Idea AUTHORED

Break every number into primes — the rest follows.

The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic says every composite number is a product of primes in exactly one way (order aside). That single fact is the master key to this whole chapter: it gives you HCF and LCM by comparing prime powers, it explains why HCF × LCM = product of two numbers, and it powers the proofs that 2​, 3​ and 5​ are irrational.

Definitions the board rewards NCERT VERBATIM

State these in NCERT’s exact words

Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 1,
“Every composite number can be expressed (factorised) as a product of primes, and this factorisation is unique, apart from the order in which the prime factors occur.”
What it means: Any composite number breaks into primes in exactly ONE way — only the order can differ. This uniqueness is what makes prime factorisation a reliable tool for HCF, LCM and irrationality proofs.
Prime divides a square
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 1,
“Let p be a prime number. If p divides a², then p divides a, where a is a positive integer.”
What it means: If a prime divides a2, it must already divide a itself. This is the engine of every irrationality proof — it lets you conclude 2∣a from 2∣a2.
Irrational number
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 1,
“a number 's' is called irrational if it cannot be written in the form p/q, where p and q are integers and q ≠ 0.”
What it means: A number you simply cannot write as one integer over another (with non-zero denominator). 2​, 3​, π are irrational; every terminating or repeating decimal is NOT.
📖Key terms — NCERT verbatim (state word-for-word)
Prime factorisation is unique
The prime factorisation of a natural number is unique, except for the order of its factors. (Ch 1, )
HCF = smallest powers
Product of the smallest power of each common prime factor in the numbers. (Ch 1, )
LCM = greatest powers
Product of the greatest power of each prime factor, involved in the numbers. (Ch 1, )
HCF × LCM = product
for any two positive integers a and b, HCF (a, b) × LCM (a, b) = a × b. (Ch 1, )
Must-know concepts AUTHORED FRAMING

…and where they’re tested

1
Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic
Every composite number splits into primes in exactly one way (order aside). Build a factor tree — keep dividing by the smallest prime — until only primes remain, then collect them as powers, e.g. 32760=23×32×5×7×13.
1-2 mark 'express as a product of primes' / composite-number reasoning
2
HCF & LCM by prime factorisation
Prime-factorise each number. HCF = product of the smallest power of each COMMON prime; LCM = product of the greatest power of EVERY prime that appears. Line the powers up in a column and read them off.
2-3 mark 'find the HCF and LCM by prime factorisation'
3
HCF × LCM = product of the two numbers
For any two positive integers, HCF(a,b)×LCM(a,b)=a×b. So once you have the HCF you can get the LCM without re-factorising: LCM=HCFa×b​. ⚑ Warning: this holds for TWO numbers only, never three.
1-2 mark 'given HCF, find LCM' (e.g. HCF(306,657)=9 → find LCM)
4
Proving irrationality by contradiction
To prove p​ irrational: assume it is rational, p​=a/b with a,b coprime. Square and rearrange to get p∣a2, so (by Theorem 1.2) p∣a; substitute back to force p∣b too. Now a and b share the factor p — contradicting 'coprime'. Hence p​ is irrational.
3-mark 'prove that √2 / √3 / √5 is irrational'
5
'Can it end in 0/5?' — uniqueness in action
A number ends in 0 only if its prime factorisation contains BOTH 2 and 5. Since 4n=22n has no factor 5, it can never end in 0. The uniqueness half of the theorem is what lets you rule this out for every n at once.
1-2 mark competency: 'check whether 6ⁿ (or 4ⁿ) can end with the digit 0'

Common mistakes that cost marks

Where students lose easy marks

✕
Claiming HCF × LCM = product for THREE numbers. It holds for two numbers only.
✕
Forgetting 'coprime' in an irrationality proof. The whole contradiction rests on assuming a,b share no factor — skip it and the proof collapses.
✕
Jumping from p∣a2 straight to a=pc without citing Theorem 1.2. The step p∣a2⇒p∣a is exactly what earns the mark.
✕
Swapping the HCF/LCM rules — HCF takes the SMALLEST powers of COMMON primes; LCM takes the GREATEST powers of ALL primes.

√2 is irrational — the proof by contradiction

This is NCERT's Theorem 1.3 (p.6-7). It is the model every irrationality proof in this chapter follows, and rests entirely on Theorem 1.2 (p∣a2⇒p∣a), which itself comes from the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic. Learn this one and √3, √5 are the same argument with a different prime.

1
Assume, to the contrary, that √2 is rational.
2​=sr​,s=0
Proof by contradiction: we suppose the negation and drive it to an impossibility.
2
Reduce to lowest terms, so the numerator and denominator are coprime.
2​=ba​,a,b coprime
Divide out any common factor to get a and b with no common factor other than 1.
3
Multiply across and square.
2b2=a2⇒2∣a2
From b√2 = a, squaring gives 2b² = a².
4
So 2 divides a.
2∣a⇒a=2c
By Theorem 1.2, since the prime 2 divides a², it divides a. Write a = 2c.
5
Substitute back — now 2 divides b as well.
b2=2c2⇒2∣b
2b² = 4c² gives b² = 2c², so 2 | b² and (again by Theorem 1.2) 2 | b.
6
Contradiction: a and b share the factor 2, yet we chose them coprime.
∴ 2​ is irrational.
The contradiction arose from assuming √2 rational — so that assumption is false.

Three cubical warehouses of volume 165 m³, 195 m³ and 285 m³ are to be used for storage. What is the volume of the greatest cubical box that can be kept in the warehouse so that no space is left vacant?

Step-marked solution
  1. [1 mark] Required volume = HCF(165, 195, 285) = 15 m³. Answer: B.

Shilpi wants to organize a party. She has 36 kiwis and 60 oranges at home and decided to distribute them equally among all. She decides to add 42 apples also. In this case, how many maximum guests can she invite?

Step-marked solution
  1. [1 mark] Maximum guests = HCF(36, 60, 42) = 6. Answer: A.
1 markSection A

Shweta wants to organize a party. She has 336 guavas and 54 oranges at home and decided to distribute them equally among all. How many maximum guests can she invite?

Step-marked solution
  1. [1 mark] 336 = 56 × 6 and 54 = 9 × 6, so HCF(336, 54) = 6 = maximum guests. Answer: A.
