Magnetic Effects of Electric Current

★

What the board actually asks: the two direction rules (right-hand thumb rule for the field around a wire, Fleming's left-hand rule for the force on a wire), the properties of magnetic field lines, the field pattern of a solenoid compared with a bar magnet, and why a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field feels a force. These are mostly 1-3 mark direction-and-reasoning questions, not numericals.

The Big Idea AUTHORED

A current makes a magnet; a magnet pushes a current.

Every electric current wraps a magnetic field around itself, and a current-carrying wire placed in someone else's magnetic field feels a sideways push. Two hand rules capture the geometry: the right-hand thumb rule tells you which way the field curls around a wire, and Fleming's left-hand rule tells you which way the force pushes the wire.

Definitions the board rewards NCERT VERBATIM

State these in NCERT’s exact words

Magnetic field
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 12,
“The region surrounding a magnet, in which the force of the magnet can be detected, is said to have a magnetic field.”
What it means: the space around a magnet where its pull or push can be felt. It is a vector — it has both a direction and a magnitude.
Magnetic field lines
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 12,
“The lines along which the iron filings align themselves represent magnetic field lines.”
What it means: the imaginary curves that map a magnetic field. A tiny compass placed on a line points along it, so a field line is the path a free north pole would follow.
Direction of the magnetic field
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 12,
“Magnetic field is a quantity that has both direction and magnitude. The direction of the magnetic field is taken to be the direction in which a north pole of the compass needle moves inside it.”
What it means: point a compass anywhere in the field — the way its north pole turns is the field direction at that spot.
Field lines are closed curves
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 12,
“Therefore it is taken by convention that the field lines emerge from north pole and merge at the south pole (note the arrows marked on the field lines in Fig. 12.4). Inside the magnet, the direction of field lines is from its south pole to its north pole. Thus the magnetic field lines are closed curves.”
What it means: outside the magnet field lines run N → S; inside they run S → N, so each line closes on itself. They never simply start or stop in mid-air.
Field strength and closeness of lines
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 12,
“The relative strength of the magnetic field is shown by the degree of closeness of the field lines. The field is stronger, that is, the force acting on the pole of another magnet placed is greater where the field lines are crowded (see Fig. 12.4).”
What it means: crowded lines mean a strong field (near the poles); widely spaced lines mean a weak field.
Right-hand thumb rule
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 12,
“Imagine that you are holding a current-carrying straight conductor in your right hand such that the thumb points towards the direction of current. Then your fingers will wrap around the conductor in the direction of the field lines of the magnetic field, as shown in Fig. 12.7. This is known as the right-hand thumb rule.”
What it means: grip the wire with your right hand, thumb along the current; your curled fingers show which way the circular field lines go around the wire.
Solenoid
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 12,
“A coil of many circular turns of insulated copper wire wrapped closely in the shape of a cylinder is called a solenoid.”
What it means: a long, tightly wound cylindrical coil. Carrying current, it behaves like a bar magnet.
Uniform field inside a solenoid
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 12,
“The field lines inside the solenoid are in the form of parallel straight lines. This indicates that the magnetic field is the same at all points inside the solenoid. That is, the field is uniform inside the solenoid.”
What it means: deep inside the coil the field is the same everywhere — same strength and direction (a uniform field).
Electromagnet
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 12,
“A strong magnetic field produced inside a solenoid can be used to magnetise a piece of magnetic material, like soft iron, when placed inside the coil (Fig. 12.11). The magnet so formed is called an electromagnet.”
What it means: put a soft-iron core inside a current-carrying solenoid and it becomes a strong magnet — an electromagnet — only while the current flows.
Fleming's left-hand rule
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 12,
“According to this rule, stretch the thumb, forefinger and middle finger of your left hand such that they are mutually perpendicular (Fig. 12.13). If the first finger points in the direction of magnetic field and the second finger in the direction of current, then the thumb will point in the direction of motion or the force acting on the conductor.”
What it means: left hand, three fingers at right angles: First finger = Field, seCond finger = Current, thuMb = Motion (force). It gives the direction of the push on the wire.
📖Key terms — NCERT verbatim (state word-for-word)
No two field lines cross
No two field-lines are found to cross each other. If they did, it would mean that at the point of intersection, the compass needle would point towards two directions, which is not possible. (Ch 12, )
Field around a straight wire
The field lines about the wire consist of a series of concentric circles whose direction is given by the right-hand rule. (Ch 12, )
When the force is largest
Experiments have shown that the displacement of the rod is largest (or the magnitude of the force is the highest) when the direction of current is at right angles to the direction of the magnetic field. (Ch 12, )
Must-know concepts AUTHORED FRAMING

…and where they’re tested

1
Magnetic field lines and their properties
Field lines picture the field: they run from N to S outside a magnet and close inside it, they are crowded where the field is strong, and no two ever cross. These four properties are the standard short-answer.
2–3 mark "list the properties of magnetic field lines" / "why don't two field lines cross?"
2
Magnetic field due to a straight current-carrying conductor
A straight wire is surrounded by concentric circular field lines. Their direction is found by the right-hand thumb rule; the field grows with the current (B∝I) and weakens with distance (B∝1/r).
2-mark "find the direction of the field" using the right-hand thumb rule
3
Field due to a circular loop and a coil
Bend the wire into a loop and every part of it pushes the field the same way through the centre, so the field there is strong and nearly straight. A coil of n turns gives n times the field of a single turn, because each turn adds up.
2-mark reasoning "why is the field stronger at the centre of a coil?"
4
Solenoid and the electromagnet
A current-carrying solenoid has a field pattern identical to a bar magnet — one end a north pole, the other a south pole — and a uniform field inside. A soft-iron core inside it becomes a strong electromagnet.
3-mark "compare the field of a solenoid with a bar magnet" / electromagnet
5
Force on a current-carrying conductor
A wire carrying current in a magnetic field feels a force perpendicular to both. The force is largest when the current is at right angles to the field, and it reverses if either the current or the field is reversed. Its direction is read off Fleming's left-hand rule.
2–3 mark "state the rule / find the direction of the force"

Example 12.1,
“A current through a horizontal power line flows in east to west direction. What is the direction of magnetic field at a point directly below it and at a point directly above it?”
1
Note the current direction.The current is along the wire, from east to west.Concept: set up the rule
2
Apply the right-hand thumb rule.Point the right thumb from east to west (along the current); the curled fingers give the field, which lies in a plane perpendicular to the wire.Concept: right-hand thumb rule
1 mark
3
Read off the direction.The magnetic field turns clockwise when viewed from the east end and anti-clockwise when viewed from the west end — so at the point below the wire and the point above it, the field points in opposite directions.Concept: below vs above
1 mark
Mark-scheme logic (authored): 1 mark for correctly applying the right-hand thumb rule, 1 mark for the clockwise/anti-clockwise sense (equivalently, that the field is oppositely directed below and above the wire).

✕
✗ Calling field lines open — they are closed curves (S→N inside the magnet).
✕
✗ Drawing two field lines that cross — impossible, since a compass can't point two ways at once.

This chapter is about DIRECTIONS, not formulas. Two hand rules do almost all the work — keep them straight, and note how the field's size changes with current and distance.

Right-hand thumb rule — direction of the field around a straight wire
1
Grip the wire with your right hand.
2
Point the thumb along the direction of the current.
3
The curled fingers give the direction of the circular field lines.
4
Reverse the current and the field direction reverses.

Fleming's left-hand rule — direction of the force on the wire

  • First fingerpoints along the magnetic Field.
  • seCond fingerpoints along the Current (conventional).
  • thuMbpoints along the Motion / force on the conductor.

Properties of magnetic field lines

  • N→SOutside the magnet lines run north to south; inside, south to north — so they are closed curves.
  • ≠No two field lines ever cross (a compass cannot point two ways).
  • ≡Crowded lines = strong field; widely spaced lines = weak field.

How the field strength changes

  • +Larger current → stronger field (B∝I).
  • −Farther from the wire → weaker field (B∝1/r).
  • ×nA coil of n turns gives n times the field of one turn.

3 competency questions from the CBSE board pattern, with the full step-marked solution for each — the marks are shown per step, as the official scheme awards them.

4 marksSection E

Two solenoids are coiled around the same non-conducting rod, one connected to a battery through a switch and the other to a voltmeter. The switch is closed. Explain why the reading on the voltmeter first increases and then returns to zero.

Step-marked solution
  1. [1 mark] Closing the switch starts a current in the primary solenoid, which produces a magnetic field through both coils.
  2. [1 mark] While the current is rising the magnetic field is changing (increasing).
  3. [1 mark] A changing magnetic field through the second coil induces an emf/voltage, so the voltmeter reading increases.
  4. [1 mark] When the current and magnetic field become steady (stop changing), no emf is induced, so the voltmeter reading returns to zero.
3 marksSection CCBSE 2024

A student fixes a sheet of white paper on a drawing board. He places a bar magnet in the centre of it. He sprinkles some iron filings uniformly around the bar magnet. Then he taps the drawing board gently and observes that the iron filings arrange themselves in a particular pattern. (a) Why do iron filings arrange in a particular pattern ? (b) What does the crowding of iron filings at the ends of the magnet indicate ? (c) What do the lines, along which the iron filings align, represent ? (d) If the student places a cardboard horizontally in a current carrying solenoid and repeats the above activity, in what pattern would the iron filings arrange ? State the conclusion drawn about the magnetic field based on the observed pattern of the lines.

Step-marked solution
  1. [1 mark] (a) Because a magnetic field exists around the bar magnet
  2. [1 mark] (b) Strength of the magnetic field is maximum near the poles of the magnet
  3. [1 mark] (c) The lines represent the magnetic field lines. (d) Equidistant parallel lines; the magnetic field inside the solenoid is uniform.

  4. [0.5 mark] OR (B) (i) Because it is a safety device, which prevents the damage to electric appliances and circuit caused due to overloading.
