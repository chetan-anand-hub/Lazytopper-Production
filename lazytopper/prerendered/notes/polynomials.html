Polynomials

★

What the board actually asks: finding the zeroes of a quadratic polynomial and verifying the sum/product relationship with its coefficients, reading the number of zeroes off a graph (the geometrical meaning), and forming a quadratic from a given sum and product of zeroes.

NCERT VERBATIM sourced, word-for-wordAUTHORED LazyTopper teaching scaffolding
The Big Idea AUTHORED

Zeroes are where the graph lands on the x-axis.

A polynomial's zeroes are the values of x that make it 0 - and geometrically, they are exactly the points where the graph y=p(x) crosses the x-axis. This short chapter ties three views of one idea together: the algebra (factorise to find the zeroes), the geometry (count the x-axis crossings), and the coefficients (for a quadratic, α+β=−ab​ and αβ=ac​). Master the quadratic case - that is what the board assesses.

Definitions the board rewards NCERT VERBATIM

State these in NCERT’s exact words

Degree of a polynomial
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 2,
“Recall that if p(x) is a polynomial in x, the highest power of x in p(x) is called the degree of the polynomial p(x).”
What it means: the highest power of the variable. 4x+2 has degree 1; 2y2−3y+4 has degree 2. Degree tells you the polynomial's family - and caps how many zeroes it can have.
Quadratic polynomial
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 2,
“More generally, any quadratic polynomial in x is of the form ax2 + bx + c, where a, b, c are real numbers and a ≠ 0.”
What it means: a degree-2 polynomial, ax2+bx+c with a=0. This is the case the whole chapter (and the board) focuses on.
Zero of a polynomial
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 2,
“More generally, a real number k is said to be a zero of a polynomial p(x), if p(k) = 0.”
What it means: a value of x that makes the polynomial equal 0. Same idea as a root of the equation p(x)=0. Note: a zero is not the value p(0).
Geometrical meaning of the zeroes
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 2,
“the zeroes of a quadratic polynomial ax2 + bx + c, a ≠ 0, are precisely the x-coordinates of the points where the parabola representing y = ax2 + bx + c intersects the x-axis.”
What it means: draw y=p(x) and look at where it meets the x-axis - each crossing is a zero. This is why the number of zeroes can be read straight off a graph.
📖Key terms — NCERT verbatim (state word-for-word)
Value p(k)
If p(x) is a polynomial in x, and if k is any real number, then the value obtained by replacing x by k in p(x), is called the value of p(x) at x = k, and is denoted by p(k). (Ch 2, )
Linear polynomial
A polynomial of degree 1 is called a linear polynomial. (Ch 2, )
Cubic polynomial
A polynomial of degree 3 is called a cubic polynomial. (Ch 2, )
Must-know concepts AUTHORED FRAMING

…and where they’re tested

1
Zeroes of a polynomial
A zero of p(x) is any value k with p(k)=0 - substitute it and the polynomial collapses to 0. For a quadratic, factorise ax2+bx+c into two linear factors and set each to zero; the two values you get are the zeroes.
1-mark 'is k a zero of p(x)?' and 2-mark 'find the zeroes'.
2
Geometrical meaning - zeroes are x-axis crossings
The zeroes of p(x) are exactly the x-coordinates where the graph y=p(x) crosses the x-axis. A parabola y=ax2+bx+c can meet the axis at two points (two distinct zeroes), touch it at one point (two equal zeroes), or miss it entirely (no zero).
1-mark MCQ 'number of zeroes from the graph' (Exercise 2.1 style).
3
A quadratic has at most two zeroes
Degree bounds the count: the graph of a degree-n polynomial meets the x-axis at most n times, so a quadratic has at most two zeroes and a linear polynomial has exactly one.
Reasoning / MCQ on the maximum number of zeroes.
4
Zeroes and coefficients (quadratic)
For zeroes α,β of ax2+bx+c: α+β=−ab​ (sum) and αβ=ac​ (product). This lets you verify computed zeroes without solving again.
2-3 mark 'find the zeroes and verify the relationship' - the chapter's staple.
5
Building a quadratic from its zeroes
Given the sum s and product p of the zeroes, a quadratic is x2−sx+p (and any real multiple k of it also works). So sum −3, product 2 gives x2+3x+2.
2-3 mark 'form the quadratic polynomial'.

Problem-solving strategies AUTHORED
→
'Find the zeroes'?Factorise the quadratic into two linear factors, set each to zero, and read off BOTH zeroes.
→
'...and verify the relationship'?Check α+β=−ab​ and αβ=ac​ - it is a separate mark, never skip it.
→
'Form a quadratic with these zeroes'?Use x2−(sum)x+(product); add that any real k-multiple also fits.
→
Graph given, 'how many zeroes'?Count the points where the curve crosses the x-axis - that is the number of zeroes.
→
'Is k a zero of p(x)?'Compute p(k); it is a zero exactly when p(k)=0.
Common mistakes that cost marks

Where students lose easy marks

✕
Giving only one zero of a quadratic - always solve both linear factors and state both values.
✕
Sign slip in the sum: α+β=−ab​ carries a minus; the product αβ=ac​ does not.
✕
Confusing a 'zero' with p(0) - a zero is where p(x)=0, not where x=0.
✕
Counting a touch-point as two crossings - when the parabola just touches the axis, that is two equal zeroes (one point), not two distinct ones.

Zeroes & coefficients of a quadratic - why sum = -b/a and product = c/a

The relationship you use to verify zeroes is not a rule to memorise blindly - NCERT derives it by writing the quadratic in terms of its factors and comparing coefficients. The 2026-27 syllabus keeps this QUADRATIC relationship (the cubic version is out of scope).

1
If α and β are the zeroes of p(x)=ax2+bx+c, then (x−α) and (x−β) are its factors.
p(x)=ax2+bx+c, a=0
2
So p(x) is a constant k times the product of those factors.
ax2+bx+c=k(x−α)(x−β)
3
Expand the right-hand side.
=kx2−k(α+β)x+kαβ
4
Compare the coefficients of x2, of x, and the constant term on both sides.
a=k,b=−k(α+β),c=kαβ
5
Divide through by k=a to isolate the sum and product.
α+β=−ab​,αβ=ac​
b/a and c/a fall straight out - this is exactly the relationship used to verify a pair of zeroes.

3 competency questions from the CBSE board pattern, with the full step-marked solution for each — the marks are shown per step, as the official scheme awards them.

5 marksSection D

One condition on the zeroes α and β of p(x) = x² − 6x + k is that the sum of their squares (α² + β²) equals 20. Determine the value of k, then obtain the two zeroes.

Step-marked solution
  1. [1 mark] For x² − 6x + k: sum of zeroes α + β = −(−6)/1 = 6 and product αβ = k/1 = k.
  2. [1 mark] Use the identity α² + β² = (α + β)² − 2αβ = 36 − 2k.
  3. [1 mark] Apply the given condition: 36 − 2k = 20.
  4. [1 mark] Solve: 2k = 16 ⇒ k = 8.
  5. [1 mark] Then p(x) = x² − 6x + 8 = (x − 2)(x − 4), zeroes 2 and 4; check 2² + 4² = 4 + 16 = 20. ✓
5 marksSection D

For p(x) = x² − 8x + k with zeroes α and β, the sum of the reciprocals of the zeroes, 1/α + 1/β, comes to 2/3. Work out k and list both zeroes.

Step-marked solution
  1. [1 mark] For x² − 8x + k: α + β = 8 and αβ = k.
  2. [1 mark] Express the condition: 1/α + 1/β = (α + β)/(αβ) = 8/k.
  3. [1 mark] So 8/k = 2/3.
  4. [1 mark] Cross-multiply: 2k = 24 ⇒ k = 12.
  5. [1 mark] Then p(x) = x² − 8x + 12 = (x − 2)(x − 6), zeroes 2 and 6; check 1/2 + 1/6 = 2/3. ✓
5 marksSection D

The zeroes α, β of p(x) = x² − 5x + k satisfy 1/α² + 1/β² = 13/36 — the sum of the reciprocals of their squares. Calculate k and identify the zeroes.

Step-marked solution
  1. [1 mark] For x² − 5x + k: α + β = 5 and αβ = k; and 1/α² + 1/β² = (α² + β²)/(αβ)².
  2. [1 mark] α² + β² = (α + β)² − 2αβ = 25 − 2k, so the expression = (25 − 2k)/k².
  3. [1 mark] Set (25 − 2k)/k² = 13/36 ⇒ 36(25 − 2k) = 13k².
  4. [1 mark] 13k² + 72k − 900 = 0 ⇒ (k − 6)(13k + 150) = 0 ⇒ k = 6 (the value giving real rational zeroes).
  5. [1 mark] Then p(x) = x² − 5x + 6 = (x − 2)(x − 3), zeroes 2 and 3; check 1/4 + 1/9 = 13/36. ✓
