Carbon and its Compounds

★

What the board actually asks: draw electron-dot (Lewis) structures for simple covalent molecules (H2, O2, N2, CH4, CO2 and compounds such as CH3Cl); give the two reasons - catenation and tetravalency - for carbon's huge number of compounds; define a homologous series and give an example; name a compound and identify its functional group; write balanced equations for combustion, the oxidation of ethanol to ethanoic acid, addition (hydrogenation), substitution, esterification and saponification, with the correct conditions above the arrow; differentiate ethanol and ethanoic acid by their physical and chemical properties; and explain the cleaning action of soap through micelle formation. Most marks sit in drawing the correct structure and writing the right reaction with its conditions.

The Big Idea AUTHORED

One element, millions of compounds - because carbon shares.

Carbon completes its octet not by giving or taking electrons but by sharing them, forming four strong covalent bonds. Two properties - catenation (carbon-to-carbon chains, branches and rings) and tetravalency - let carbon build an enormous family of compounds. Attach a functional group and a whole homologous series behaves alike, so learning one member teaches you the rest. The chapter's reactions - combustion, oxidation, addition, substitution, esterification and saponification - and the cleaning action of soap all follow from this covalent bonding.

Definitions the board rewards NCERT VERBATIM

State these in NCERT’s exact words

Covalent bond
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 4,
“Such bonds which are formed by the sharing of an electron pair between two atoms are known as covalent bonds.”
What it means: A bond made by two atoms sharing a pair of electrons so that both attain a filled outer shell - no ions are formed, which is why carbon compounds are poor conductors.
Catenation
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 4,
“Carbon has the unique ability to form bonds with other atoms of carbon, giving rise to large molecules. This property is called catenation.”
What it means: Carbon's ability to bond to other carbon atoms to build long chains, branched chains or rings; the strong C-C bond makes these compounds stable.
Saturated compounds
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 4,
“Compounds of carbon, which are linked by only single bonds between the carbon atoms are called saturated compounds.”
What it means: Carbon compounds with only single C-C bonds (the alkanes); they are generally not very reactive.
Unsaturated compounds
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 4,
“Compounds of carbon having double or triple bonds between their carbon atoms are called unsaturated compounds.”
What it means: Carbon compounds with at least one C=C or C-triple-C bond (alkenes and alkynes); they are more reactive than saturated compounds.
Hydrocarbons
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 4,
“All these carbon compounds which contain only carbon and hydrogen are called hydrocarbons.”
What it means: Compounds of only carbon and hydrogen; the saturated ones are alkanes, the unsaturated ones alkenes (double bond) or alkynes (triple bond).
Functional group
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 4,
“These heteroatoms and the group containing these confer specific properties to the compound, regardless of the length and nature of the carbon chain and hence are called functional groups.”
What it means: An atom or group of atoms (like -OH, -CHO, -COOH) that decides the chemical properties of the compound, whatever the length of the carbon chain.
Homologous series
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 4,
“Hence, such a series of compounds in which the same functional group substitutes for hydrogen in a carbon chain is called a homologous series.”
What it means: A family of compounds with the same functional group where each member differs from the next by a -CH2- unit (14 u); members share similar chemical properties and show a gradation in physical properties.

Must-know concepts AUTHORED FRAMING

…and where they’re tested

1
Covalent bonding and electron-dot structures
Carbon has four valence electrons and cannot easily gain or lose four, so it shares them, forming four covalent bonds. Show only valence electrons as dots or crosses: a single shared pair is a single bond (H2, CH4), two shared pairs a double bond (O2), three shared pairs a triple bond (N2). Because no ions form, covalent compounds have low melting/boiling points and are poor conductors of electricity.
3-mark: draw the electron dot structure of a given molecule (e.g. CH3Cl, CO2, ethanoic acid) and explain the covalent bonding.
2
Allotropes of carbon
Carbon occurs in different forms with the same chemistry but very different physical properties. In diamond each carbon is bonded to four other carbons in a rigid three-dimensional network, making it the hardest natural substance. In graphite each carbon is bonded to three others in hexagonal layers, so it is soft, slippery and (unusually for a non-metal) a good conductor. Fullerenes are a third class - C-60 has carbon atoms arranged in a football shape.
2-mark: compare the structure and one physical property of diamond and graphite; name the C-60 allotrope.
3
Versatile nature of carbon (catenation + tetravalency)
Two features explain why millions of carbon compounds exist. Catenation is carbon's ability to bond to other carbon atoms to form long chains, branched chains and rings; the C-C bond is strong and stable because carbon is small. Tetravalency means each carbon can bond to four other atoms - of carbon or of oxygen, hydrogen, nitrogen, sulphur, chlorine and more - giving compounds with many different properties.
2-mark: state the two properties of carbon that lead to the huge number of carbon compounds.
4
Chains, branches, rings and isomers
Saturated hydrocarbons (alkanes, methane to hexane) have only single bonds; unsaturated ones have double bonds (alkenes) or triple bonds (alkynes). Carbon skeletons can be straight chains, branched chains or rings (e.g. cyclohexane, benzene). From four carbons onward the same molecular formula can be arranged in more than one way - these are structural isomers (e.g. two isomers of butane, C4H10).
3-mark: draw the structures/structural isomers of a named compound (e.g. the isomers of pentane) and classify as saturated or unsaturated.
5
Homologous series, functional groups and naming
Replacing a hydrogen on a carbon chain with a functional group (halo-, alcohol -OH, aldehyde, ketone, carboxylic acid) fixes the chemistry of the compound. A homologous series shares one functional group, with successive members differing by a -CH2- unit (14 u); chemical properties stay similar while melting/boiling points rise gradually. To name a compound: count the carbons for the stem (meth-, eth-, prop-, but-...), then add the group's prefix or suffix, dropping the final 'e' before a vowel suffix (propane - e + one = propanone).
3-mark: define a homologous series with an example; name compounds or identify the functional group from a formula.
6
Chemical properties of carbon compounds
Combustion: carbon and its compounds burn in oxygen to give carbon dioxide (and water) with heat and light; saturated hydrocarbons give a clean blue flame, unsaturated ones a sooty yellow flame. Oxidation: alcohols are oxidised to carboxylic acids by oxidising agents (alkaline KMnO4 or acidified K2Cr2O7). Addition: unsaturated hydrocarbons add hydrogen over a nickel/palladium catalyst (hydrogenation). Substitution: in sunlight, chlorine replaces the hydrogens of a saturated hydrocarbon one by one.
identify the reaction type from an equation; name the products; state which hydrocarbons undergo addition reactions.
7
Ethanol, ethanoic acid, soaps and detergents
Ethanol (alcohol) reacts with sodium to release hydrogen and, with hot concentrated sulphuric acid, dehydrates to ethene. Ethanoic acid (acetic acid, a weak carboxylic acid; its pure form is glacial acetic acid) forms an ester with ethanol (esterification), gives a salt and water with a base, and releases carbon dioxide with carbonates and hydrogencarbonates. Soaps are sodium/potassium salts of long-chain carboxylic acids; their molecules form micelles that trap oily dirt (ionic heads out, hydrocarbon tails in) so it rinses away, while detergents keep working even in hard water.
3-mark: explain the cleaning action of soap via micelle formation; differentiate ethanol and ethanoic acid by their properties.

Problem-solving strategies AUTHORED
→
Drawing an electron dot structure?Show only the valence electrons and give every atom a completely filled outer shell. Carbon is tetravalent, so it forms four shared pairs; one shared pair is a single bond, two pairs a double bond, three pairs a triple bond.
→
Naming a compound?First count the carbon atoms for the stem (meth-1, eth-2, prop-3, but-4...), then add the functional group as a prefix or suffix. Drop the final 'e' of the stem before a suffix beginning with a vowel (propane - e + one = propanone).
→
Which reaction type is it?Saturated + Cl2 in sunlight is substitution; unsaturated + H2 over Ni is addition; alcohol + oxidising agent is oxidation; acid + alcohol is esterification; ester + NaOH is saponification.
→
Cleaning action asked?Explain the soap molecule's two ends (an ionic hydrophilic head and a hydrocarbon hydrophobic tail), how micelles form around oily dirt, and how agitation lifts the emulsified dirt into the water so it rinses away.
Common mistakes that cost marks

Where students lose easy marks

✕
Changing subscripts instead of coefficients to balance a combustion equation - only the coefficients in front of a formula may be changed, never the subscripts inside it.
✕
Confusing addition (hydrogen adds across the double/triple bond of an unsaturated compound) with substitution (an atom replaces a hydrogen in a saturated compound).
✕
Forgetting to drop the final 'e' of the stem before a vowel suffix - e.g. writing 'propaneone' instead of propanone.
✕
Claiming a micelle forms in any solvent. It forms in water because one end is hydrophilic; in a pure hydrocarbon solvent no micelle forms.
✕
Calling ethanoic acid a strong acid. Carboxylic acids are weak acids and are only partially ionised, unlike mineral acids such as HCl.

3 competency questions from the CBSE board pattern, with the full step-marked solution for each — the marks are shown per step, as the official scheme awards them.

5 marksSection D

A carbon compound P has six carbon atoms and twelve hydrogen atoms. (a) Is P a saturated or unsaturated carbon compound? Justify by drawing the structural formula. (b) Describe a test that can be used to determine if compound P is saturated or unsaturated. (c) Name the products formed on burning compound P in excess of air.

Step-marked solution
  1. [1 mark] (a) C6H12 fits the general formula CnH2n, which is satisfied both by unsaturated alkenes (one C=C double bond) and by saturated cycloalkanes (a ring). So P may be saturated or unsaturated.
  2. [1 mark] (a) Structures: unsaturated hexene CH3-CH=CH-CH2-CH2-CH3 (C4=C bond) and saturated cyclohexane (a six-membered ring of six CH2 groups).
  3. [1 mark] (b) Burning test: an unsaturated compound burns with a sooty yellow flame (high C/H ratio), while a saturated compound burns with a clean blue flame.
  4. [1 mark] (b) Bromine-water test: an unsaturated compound decolourises brown bromine water; a saturated compound does not.
  5. [1 mark] (c) On complete combustion in excess air, any hydrocarbon gives carbon dioxide (CO2) and water (H2O).
4 marksSection E

Given a 4-carbon skeleton of a hydrocarbon compound. (a) Fill in the hydrogen atoms/bonds to form: (i) a saturated hydrocarbon (ii) an unsaturated hydrocarbon. (b) If the four-carbon skeleton is of a straight chained alkene, draw the structures of all the possible compounds.

Step-marked solution
  1. [1 mark] (a)(i) Saturated hydrocarbon with 4 carbons: butane, CH3-CH2-CH2-CH3 (C4H10), with all single bonds.
  2. [1 mark] (a)(ii) Unsaturated hydrocarbon: butene CH2=CH-CH2-CH3 (C4H8) with a C=C double bond (or butyne CH≡C-CH2-CH3, C4H6).
  3. [1 mark] (b) First straight-chain alkene: But-1-ene, CH2=CH-CH2-CH3, with the double bond at position 1.
  4. [1 mark] (b) Second straight-chain alkene: But-2-ene, CH3-CH=CH-CH3, with the double bond at position 2.
4 marksSection E

If the four-carbon skeleton is of a straight chained alkyne: (i) How many carbon atoms may NOT be bonded to any hydrogen atoms? (ii) How many hydrogen atoms will there be in the compound?

Step-marked solution
  1. [1 mark] The straight-chain alkynes with 4 carbons are but-1-yne (HC≡C-CH2-CH3) and but-2-yne (CH3-C≡C-CH3), both C4H6.
  2. [1 mark] (i) In but-1-yne, HC≡C-CH2-CH3: C1 has 1 H, C2 has 0 H, C3 has 2 H, C4 has 3 H → one carbon (C2) is bonded to no hydrogen.
  3. [1 mark] (i) In but-2-yne, CH3-C≡C-CH3: C1 has 3 H, C2 has 0 H, C3 has 0 H, C4 has 3 H → two carbons (C2 and C3) are bonded to no hydrogen. So one or two carbons may not be bonded to any H.
  4. [1 mark] (ii) The molecular formula is C4H6, so the compound contains six hydrogen atoms.
