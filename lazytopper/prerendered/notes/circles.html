Circles

★

What the board actually asks: the two tangent theorems — the tangent is perpendicular to the radius at the point of contact (Theorem 10.1) and the two tangents from an external point are equal (Theorem 10.2) — plus “find the length of the tangent / radius” numericals and circumscribed-figure proofs. Almost every mark here rides on those two theorems.

The Big Idea AUTHORED

One point of contact, one right angle.

A tangent touches a circle at exactly one point; a secant cuts it at two. The whole chapter hangs on two facts: at the point of contact the tangent is perpendicular to the radius (Theorem 10.1), and from a point outside the circle you can draw exactly two tangents whose lengths are equal (Theorem 10.2). Together they turn tangent questions into right-triangle and congruence problems you already know how to solve.

Definitions the board rewards NCERT VERBATIM

State these in NCERT’s exact words

Tangent to a circle
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 10,
“a tangent to a circle is a line that intersects the circle at only one point.”
What it means: A line that grazes the circle at a single point of contact. Contrast a secant (2 common points) and a non-intersecting line (0). The word comes from the Latin tangere, “to touch”.
Secant of a circle
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 10,
“there are two common points A and B that the line PQ and the circle have. In this case, we call the line PQ a secant of the circle.”
What it means: A line that cuts the circle at two points. Slide a secant until its two crossing points merge into one and it becomes a tangent — that limiting idea is the bridge to the whole chapter.
Theorem 10.1 — tangent ⊥ radius
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 10,
“The tangent at any point of a circle is perpendicular to the radius through the point of contact.”
What it means: At the point of contact, radius ⊥ tangent — a guaranteed 90°. This right angle is the workhorse of almost every numerical and proof in the chapter (it hands you a right triangle for Pythagoras).
Theorem 10.2 — equal tangents from an external point
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 10,
“The lengths of tangents drawn from an external point to a circle are equal.”
What it means: From a point outside, the two tangent segments are equal (PQ=PR). This is what powers the circumscribed-quadrilateral and inscribed-circle proofs; the centre also lies on the bisector of the angle between the two tangents.
Length of the tangent
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 10,
“The length of the segment of the tangent from the external point P and the point of contact with the circle is called the length of the tangent from the point P to the circle.”
What it means: The distance from the external point to where the tangent touches the circle. With radius ⊥ tangent it is the leg of a right triangle, so ℓ=d2−r2​ (d = distance of the point from the centre, r = radius).

Must-know concepts AUTHORED FRAMING

…and where they’re tested

1
A line and a circle — three positions
Given a circle and a line, exactly three things can happen: the line misses the circle (non-intersecting, 0 common points), cuts it at two points (secant), or touches it at one point (tangent). A tangent is the limiting case of a secant whose two crossing points have merged.
1-mark fill-in-the-blank / MCQ (Exercise 10.1 Q2)
2
Tangent ⊥ radius (Theorem 10.1)
At the point of contact, the tangent is perpendicular to the radius. Two consequences NCERT flags in the Remarks: there is one and only one tangent at a point of a circle, and the line along that radius is called the normal at the point.
cited inside almost every circle proof; the 90° carries the mark
3
How many tangents from a point? 0, 1 or 2
From a point inside the circle you can draw no tangent; from a point on the circle exactly one; from a point outside exactly two. State which case you are in before you start computing.
1-mark conceptual; sets up the length-of-tangent numericals
4
Equal tangents from an external point (Theorem 10.2)
The two tangents from an outside point have equal length (PQ=PR), so they form an isosceles triangle and the centre lies on the bisector of the angle between them. Labelling equal tangent segments from each vertex is the key move in circumscribed-figure proofs.
circumscribed quadrilateral/triangle proofs, e.g. AB+CD=AD+BC — Sections C/D
5
Length of a tangent — the right-triangle numerical
Because radius ⊥ tangent, the radius, the tangent segment and the line from the point to the centre form a right triangle. Pythagoras gives the length of the tangent ℓ=d2−r2​, where d is the distance of the point from the centre and r the radius.
2–3 mark “find the tangent length / find the radius” (Exercise 10.2 Q1, Q6)

Problem-solving strategies AUTHORED
→
Tangent + radius in the figure?Mark the point of contact and draw the radius to it — it is ⊥ to the tangent (Theorem 10.1). That 90° unlocks Pythagoras or a right triangle.
→
Two tangents from one outside point?They are equal (Theorem 10.2). Equal tangents ⇒ an isosceles triangle, and the centre sits on the bisector of the angle between them.
→
Find a tangent length, radius or distance?Build the radius–tangent–(line-to-centre) right triangle, then apply Pythagoras: (tangent)2=(distance to centre)2−(radius)2.
→
Circle inscribed in a quadrilateral or triangle?Label the equal tangent segments from each vertex; the opposite-side sums come out equal, e.g. AB+CD=AD+BC.
→
How many tangents?Inside → 0, on the circle → 1, outside → 2. State the case before you compute.
Common mistakes that cost marks

Where students lose easy marks

✕
Dropping the perpendicular to the wrong point — the tangent is ⊥ to the radius only at the point of contact, not at any other point of the circle.
✕
Adding instead of subtracting in the tangent right triangle: (tangent)2=OQ2−OP2, so PQ=d2−r2​, never d2+r2​.
✕
Skipping the theorem citation in a proof — stating “the tangents are equal” without invoking Theorem 10.2 (or “radius ⊥ tangent” without Theorem 10.1) forfeits the reasoning mark.
✕
Forgetting the isosceles step in an angle problem — equal tangents make the triangle isosceles; without that, the base-angle relation and the whole angle-chase collapse.

Theorem 10.2 — the lengths of tangents from an external point are equal

The chapter’s workhorse result, proved by RHS congruence of the two right triangles the radii create. NCERT’s Remarks add two facts: the same result follows from the Pythagoras Theorem (PQ2=OP2−OQ2=OP2−OR2=PR2), and since ∠OPQ=∠OPR, the centre lies on the bisector of the angle between the two tangents.

1
Set up what is given and what to prove.
A circle with centre O, a point P outside it, and two tangents PQ, PR from P. Required: PQ = PR.
2
Join OP, OQ and OR.
OQ and OR are radii to the points of contact Q and R.
3
The radius–tangent angles are right angles.
∠OQP=∠ORP=90°
Each is the angle between a radius and the tangent at its point of contact, so by Theorem 10.1 they are right angles.
4
Compare right triangles OQP and ORP.
OQ=OR,OP=OP
OQ = OR (radii of the same circle) and OP = OP (common hypotenuse).
5
The triangles are congruent.
△OQP≅△ORP
Right angle, hypotenuse and one side equal — RHS congruence.
6
Hence the tangent lengths are equal.
PQ=PR
PQ and PR are corresponding parts of the congruent triangles (CPCT).

3 competency questions from the CBSE board pattern, with the full step-marked solution for each — the marks are shown per step, as the official scheme awards them.

5 marksSection D

In an amusement park, a triangular path ABC circumscribing a circular pond centred at O with radius 8 m is to be constructed, as shown in the figure. The tangent lengths from B are 12 m and from C are 16 m. Find the cost of fencing the triangular path at the rate of Rs 55 per metre.

Step-marked solution
  1. [1 mark] Apply the circle theorems: tangent lengths from an external point are equal, so PB = QB = 12 m, RC = QC = 16 m and AP = AR = x (say); also the radius (8 m) is perpendicular to each tangent at the point of contact.
  2. [1 mark] Find ar(△ABC) as the sum of areas of △AOB, △BOC and △AOC: ar(△ABC) = ½ × 8 × (perimeter) = ½ × 8 × 2(28 + x) = 8(28 + x) sq m. … (1)
  3. [1 mark] Using Heron's formula with semi-perimeter s = (28 + x): ar(△ABC) = √[s(s−a)(s−b)(s−c)] = √[(28 + x) · x · 12 · 16] = 4√[(28 + x) · x · 12] sq m. … (2)
  4. [1 mark] Equate (1) and (2): 8(28 + x) = 4√[(28 + x) · x · 12]; squaring gives 4(28 + x)² = 12x(28 + x) → (28 + x)(28 + x − 3x) = 0 → x = 14 (reject x = −28). So perimeter = 2(28 + x) = 84 m.
  5. [1 mark] Cost of fencing = Rs 55 × 84 = Rs 4620.
5 marksSection D

From an external point P, two tangents PA and PB are drawn to a circle with centre O. At a point E on the circle, the tangent intersects PA at C and PB at D. If PA = 10 cm, find the perimeter of triangle PCD.

Step-marked solution
  1. [1 mark] Tangents drawn from an external point are equal, so PA = PB = 10 cm.
  2. [1 mark] From C the two tangents give CA = CE; from D they give DB = DE (equal tangents from an external point).
  3. [1 mark] Perimeter(△PCD) = PC + CD + DP = PC + (CE + ED) + DP.
  4. [1 mark] Substitute CE = CA and ED = DB: = (PC + CA) + (DP + DB) = PA + PB.
  5. [1 mark] Perimeter = 10 + 10 = 20 cm. Hence the perimeter of △PCD is 20 cm.
5 marksSection DCBSE 2026

PQ and PR are two tangents to a circle with centre O and radius 5 cm. AB is another tangent to the circle at C which lies on OP. If OP = 13 cm, then find the length AB and PA.

Step-marked solution
  1. [1 mark] Given: circle with centre O and radius OQ = 5 cm, external point P with OP = 13 cm; PQ and PR are tangents from P, so PQ = PR and OQ ⊥ PQ.
  2. [1 mark] Tangent length from P: PQ = √(OP² − OQ²) = √(169 − 25) = √144 = 12 cm.
  3. [1 mark] C lies on OP and AB is the tangent at C, so OC = radius = 5 cm ⇒ PC = OP − OC = 13 − 5 = 8 cm, and AB ⊥ OP at C.
  4. [1 mark] Let AC = AQ = x (equal tangents from A). Then PA = PQ − AQ = 12 − x. In right △PCA (right angle at C): PA² = PC² + AC² ⇒ (12 − x)² = 8² + x² ⇒ 144 − 24x = 64 ⇒ x = 10/3.
  5. [1 mark] Hence AC = 10/3 cm, so AB = 2·AC = 20/3 cm ≈ 6.67 cm and PA = 12 − 10/3 = 26/3 cm ≈ 8.67 cm.
