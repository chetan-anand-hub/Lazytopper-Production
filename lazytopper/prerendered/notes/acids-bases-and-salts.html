Acids, Bases and Salts

★

What the board actually asks: identify an acid or a base with indicators (blue/red litmus, methyl orange, phenolphthalein, and olfactory indicators); write and balance the reactions of acids and bases - with metals (Salt + Hydrogen), with metal carbonates/hydrogencarbonates (Salt + Carbon dioxide + Water), acid-base neutralisation (Salt + Water), and metal-oxide / non-metal-oxide reactions; explain what happens to an acid or a base in water (H+(aq)/H3O+ and OH-(aq) ions) and why their solutions conduct electricity; use the pH scale (0-14) and its everyday importance (body pH, soil, digestion and antacids, tooth decay, acid rain); classify salts by family and by the pH of the salt; and give the preparation, properties and uses of the chlor-alkali products, washing soda, baking soda, bleaching powder and plaster of Paris (water of crystallisation). Most marks sit in writing the correct balanced reaction and in the pH reasoning.

The Big Idea AUTHORED

Sour or bitter - decoded by H+, OH- and pH.

Acids taste sour and turn blue litmus red; bases taste bitter and turn red litmus blue. In water an acid releases hydrogen ions, H+(aq) (shown as the hydronium ion H3O+), and a base releases hydroxide ions, OH-(aq) - and those ions explain everything else: why acid and base solutions conduct electricity, how they react with metals, carbonates and each other, and where a solution sits on the pH scale from 0 to 14. When an acid neutralises a base you get a salt plus water, and the chapter then follows one salt - common salt - as it becomes sodium hydroxide, bleaching powder, baking soda and washing soda, then closes with plaster of Paris (made from gypsum).

Definitions the board rewards NCERT VERBATIM

State these in NCERT’s exact words

Acid-base indicators
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 2,
“Acid-base indicators are dyes or mixtures of dyes which are used to indicate the presence of acids and bases.”
What it means: Substances that signal whether something is acidic or basic by changing colour - litmus, methyl orange and phenolphthalein are the common ones.
Olfactory indicators
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 2,
“There are some substances whose odour changes in acidic or basic media. These are called olfactory indicators.”
What it means: Indicators you smell rather than see - onion and clove oil lose their smell in one medium, so they can flag an acid or a base.
Neutralisation reaction
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 2,
“The reaction between an acid and a base to give a salt and water is known as a neutralisation reaction.”
What it means: Acid and base cancel each other out, producing a salt and water: Acid + Base -> Salt + Water.
Alkali
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 2,
“Bases which are soluble in water are called alkalis.”
What it means: A base that dissolves in water - like NaOH or KOH. All alkalis are bases, but not every base is an alkali.
pH scale
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 2,
“A scale for measuring hydrogen ion concentration in a solution, called pH scale has been developed.”
What it means: A 0-14 number line for how acidic or basic a solution is: 0 very acidic, 7 neutral, 14 very alkaline. The higher the H3O+ concentration, the lower the pH.
Strong and weak acids
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 2,
“Acids that give rise to more H+ ions are said to be strong acids, and acids that give less H+ ions are said to be weak acids.”
What it means: Strength is about how fully an acid ionises, not how dilute it is - a strong acid produces more H+ ions than a weak acid of the same concentration.
pH of salts
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 2,
“Salts of a strong acid and a strong base are neutral with pH value of 7. On the other hand, salts of a strong acid and weak base are acidic with pH value less than 7 and those of a strong base and weak acid are basic in nature, with pH value more than 7.”
What it means: The salt inherits the stronger partner: strong+strong = neutral (7); strong acid + weak base = acidic (<7); strong base + weak acid = basic (>7).
Water of crystallisation
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 2,
“Water of crystallisation is the fixed number of water molecules present in one formula unit of a salt.”
What it means: The water locked into a crystal - CuSO4.5H2O has five, Na2CO3.10H2O has ten; heating drives it off (blue copper sulphate turns white).
Dilution
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 2,
“Mixing an acid or base with water results in decrease in the concentration of ions (H3O+/OH–) per unit volume. Such a process is called dilution and the acid or the base is said to be diluted.”
What it means: Adding water lowers the ion concentration. Always add the acid to water slowly with stirring - the process is highly exothermic.

Must-know concepts AUTHORED FRAMING

…and where they’re tested

1
Testing acids and bases with indicators
Indicators change colour (or odour) in acidic and basic media. Blue litmus turns red in an acid; red litmus turns blue in a base. Methyl orange and phenolphthalein are synthetic indicators (phenolphthalein is colourless in acid, pink in base), and substances whose smell changes - onion, vanilla and clove oil - are olfactory indicators.
1-2 mark: name an indicator and its colour change; identify three test tubes (acid / base / distilled water) using only red litmus paper.
2
Reactions of acids and bases
Acids react with metals to give a salt and hydrogen gas; with metal carbonates and hydrogencarbonates to give a salt, carbon dioxide and water; and with bases in a neutralisation reaction to give a salt and water. Metal oxides are basic (they react with acids to give salt and water), while non-metal oxides are acidic (they react with bases).
3-5 mark: write and balance the reaction, name the salt and the gas, and describe the test for the gas evolved.
3
What all acids and all bases have in common
All acids produce hydrogen ions, H+(aq) - shown as the hydronium ion H3O+ - but only in the presence of water; all bases produce hydroxide ions, OH-(aq). These free ions carry current, so acid and base solutions conduct electricity, whereas glucose and alcohol contain hydrogen yet do not ionise and do not conduct.
2 mark: why dry HCl gas does not turn dry litmus red; why an aqueous acid conducts electricity while glucose solution does not.
4
Strength and the pH scale
Strength depends on how many H+ (or OH-) ions a solution produces, not on how concentrated it is - a strong acid ionises more fully than a weak acid at the same concentration. The universal indicator and the pH scale (0 very acidic, 7 neutral, 14 very alkaline) measure hydrogen-ion concentration: the higher the H3O+ concentration, the lower the pH.
2-3 mark: order solutions by pH / H+ concentration; classify solutions as strong or weak, acidic or basic.
5
Importance of pH in everyday life
Living things survive in a narrow pH band (our body works around 7.0-7.8). Soil needs a suitable pH for healthy plants; the stomach uses hydrochloric acid for digestion, and antacids (mild bases such as milk of magnesia) neutralise excess acid; tooth decay begins when the mouth pH falls below 5.5; rain below pH 5.6 is acid rain. Bee and nettle stings are acidic and are soothed with a mild base like baking soda.
2-3 mark: why antacids relieve indigestion; why tooth-cleaning is basic; acid rain / treating soil with lime.
6
Salts - family and pH
A salt is formed when an acid neutralises a base. Salts with the same positive or negative radical belong to one family (NaCl and Na2SO4 are sodium salts; NaCl and KCl are chloride salts). A salt of a strong acid and a strong base is neutral (pH 7); a strong-acid / weak-base salt is acidic (pH < 7); and a strong-base / weak-acid salt is basic (pH > 7).
identify the acid and base a salt came from; predict whether the salt is acidic, basic or neutral.
7
Chemicals from common salt, and water of crystallisation
Common salt (NaCl) is the raw material for many chemicals. Electrolysis of brine - the chlor-alkali process - gives sodium hydroxide, chlorine and hydrogen. Chlorine on dry slaked lime gives bleaching powder, Ca(ClO)2. Baking soda (NaHCO3) and washing soda (Na2CO3.10H2O) are both made from common salt, and heating gypsum at 373 K gives plaster of Paris. Water of crystallisation is the fixed number of water molecules in one formula unit of a salt (CuSO4.5H2O, Na2CO3.10H2O, CaSO4.2H2O).
3-5 mark: uses of the chlor-alkali products; equations for baking soda, washing soda and plaster of Paris; what 10H2O signifies.

Problem-solving strategies AUTHORED
→
Acid or base?Reach for an indicator first - blue litmus red = acid, red litmus blue = base; phenolphthalein stays colourless in acid and turns pink in base.
→
Reaction asked?Match the second reactant: metal -> Salt + H2; carbonate / hydrogencarbonate -> Salt + CO2 + Water; base -> Salt + Water (neutralisation); metal oxide is basic, non-metal oxide is acidic. Then balance and add state symbols.
→
Test the gas.Hydrogen burns with a pop sound; carbon dioxide turns lime water milky and puts out a flame - name the gas by its test.
→
pH question?Lower pH means more H+ and more acidic; exactly 7 is neutral. To compare two solutions, the one with the lower pH has the higher hydrogen-ion concentration.
→
A chemical from common salt?Anchor each to NaCl: brine -> NaOH + Cl2 + H2 (chlor-alkali); Cl2 + dry slaked lime -> bleaching powder; heat gypsum -> plaster of Paris.
Common mistakes that cost marks

Where students lose easy marks

✕
Thinking 'salt' means only sodium chloride. In chemistry a salt is any product of an acid neutralising a base - ZnSO4, CaCl2 and Na2CO3 are all salts.
✕
Forgetting that H+ ions form only in water, so dry HCl gas does NOT turn dry litmus red - the separation of H+ from the HCl molecule needs water.
✕
Confusing strength with concentration. A strong acid ionises more fully than a weak acid at the same concentration; strong / weak is about the number of H+ ions produced, not how dilute the solution is.
✕
Adding water to a concentrated acid. Always add the acid to water slowly with stirring - dissolving an acid or base is highly exothermic and the mixture can splash out.
✕
Mixing up baking soda and washing soda - baking soda is NaHCO3 (sodium hydrogencarbonate) while washing soda is Na2CO3.10H2O (hydrated sodium carbonate).
✕
Writing plaster of Paris with a whole water molecule. It is calcium sulphate hemihydrate, CaSO4.(1/2)H2O - two formula units share one water molecule.

3 competency questions from the CBSE board pattern, with the full step-marked solution for each — the marks are shown per step, as the official scheme awards them.

5 marksSection D

In the following schematic diagram for the preparation of hydrogen gas (Figure 2.3), what would happen if following changes are made? (a) In place of zinc granules, same amount of zinc dust is taken in the test tube (b) Instead of dilute sulphuric acid, dilute hydrochloric acid is taken (c) In place of zinc, copper turnings are taken (d) Sodium hydroxide is taken in place of dilute sulphuric acid and the tube is heated.

Step-marked solution
  1. [1 mark] (a) Smaller particle size = larger surface area → faster reaction. H₂ evolves more rapidly.
  2. [1 mark] (b) Dilute HCl is a strong acid, Zn above H → Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂. Same effect — H₂ evolved.
  3. [1 mark] (c) Cu is BELOW H in the reactivity series → Cu cannot displace H from dilute acids. No H₂ evolved, no reaction.
  4. [1 mark] (d) Zinc is amphoteric (reacts with both acid and strong base). With hot NaOH: Zn(s) + 2NaOH(aq) → Na₂ZnO₂(aq) + H₂(g)↑. H₂ is evolved.
  5. [1 mark] Summary: (a) faster; (b) yes H₂; (c) no reaction; (d) yes H₂ (different reaction with sodium zincate formation).
5 marksSection D

For making cake, baking powder is taken. If at home your mother uses baking soda instead of baking powder in cake, (a) how will it affect the taste of the cake and why? (b) how can baking soda be converted into baking powder? (c) what is the role of tartaric acid added to baking soda?

Step-marked solution
  1. [1 mark] (a) Baking soda alone in cake: heating → 2NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂. The leftover Na₂CO₃ is basic and tastes bitter. So cake becomes bitter.
  2. [1 mark] (b) Baking powder = baking soda (NaHCO₃) + a mild edible acid (tartaric acid).
  3. [1 mark] (c) Tartaric acid neutralises Na₂CO₃ formed on heating: Na₂CO₃ + tartaric acid → sodium tartarate (tasteless salt) + H₂O + CO₂.
  4. [1 mark] (c) This prevents bitterness AND releases additional CO₂ → cake rises and becomes spongy.
  5. [1 mark] (c) Overall, tartaric acid plays a dual role: taste correction + extra leavening.
5 marksSection D

A metal carbonate X on reacting with an acid gives a gas which when passed through a solution Y gives the carbonate back. On the other hand, a gas G that is obtained at anode during electrolysis of brine is passed on dry Y, it gives a compound Z, used for disinfecting drinking water. Identify X, Y, G and Z.

Step-marked solution
  1. [1 mark] Gas from metal carbonate + acid = CO₂.
  2. [1 mark] CO₂ + Y gives carbonate back → Y is Ca(OH)₂ (lime water): CO₂ + Ca(OH)₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O. The carbonate formed is CaCO₃ → so X = CaCO₃.
  3. [0.5 mark] Electrolysis of brine: anode gives Cl₂, cathode gives H₂. So G = Cl₂.
  4. [0.5 mark] Cl₂ + dry slaked lime: Ca(OH)₂ + Cl₂ → CaOCl₂ + H₂O. Z = CaOCl₂.
  5. [1 mark] CaOCl₂ is bleaching powder, used as a disinfectant for drinking water.
  6. [1 mark] Summary: X = CaCO₃, Y = Ca(OH)₂, G = Cl₂, Z = CaOCl₂.
