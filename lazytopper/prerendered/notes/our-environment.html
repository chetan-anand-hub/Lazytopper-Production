The Big Idea AUTHORED

Everything is linked - energy flows one way, matter cycles, and what we dump comes back to us.

An ecosystem is living organisms plus their non-living surroundings interacting as one balanced unit. Energy from the Sun enters through green producers and passes up the food chain, but only about a tenth carries over at each step, so food chains stay short. The same chains carry our mistakes upward - non-degradable pesticides concentrate in the top consumer (us), CFCs thin the protective ozone layer, and non-biodegradable waste piles up. Most marks here reward clear reasoning and the exact NCERT terms, not long descriptions.

Definitions the board rewards NCERT VERBATIM

State these in NCERT’s exact words

Ecosystem
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 13,
“All the interacting organisms in an area together with the non-living constituents of the environment form an ecosystem. Thus, an ecosystem consists of biotic components comprising living organisms and abiotic components comprising physical factors like temperature, rainfall, wind, soil and minerals.”
What it means: A community of living things (biotic) plus their non-living surroundings (abiotic) interacting as one balanced unit - a garden, forest, pond or aquarium.
Producers
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 13,
“All green plants and certain bacteria which can produce food by photosynthesis come under this category and are called the producers.”
What it means: Green plants and some bacteria that make their own food by photosynthesis - the first link that brings energy into every food chain.
Consumers
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 13,
“These organisms which consume the food produced, either directly from producers or indirectly by feeding on other consumers are the consumers.”
What it means: Organisms that cannot make their own food and instead eat producers or other consumers - classed as herbivores, carnivores, omnivores and parasites.
Decomposers
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 13,
“These microorganisms are the decomposers as they break-down the complex organic substances into simple inorganic substances that go into the soil and are used up once more by the plants.”
What it means: Bacteria and fungi that break down dead remains and wastes into simple substances returned to the soil, so nutrients are recycled to the producers.
Trophic level
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 13,
“Each step or level of the food chain forms a trophic level.”
What it means: Each feeding step of a food chain - producers are the first level, primary consumers the second, secondary the third and tertiary the fourth.
Biological magnification
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 13,
“As human beings occupy the top level in any food chain, the maximum concentration of these chemicals get accumulated in our bodies. This phenomenon is known as biological magnification.”
What it means: The progressive build-up of non-degradable chemicals (like pesticides) at each higher trophic level, reaching its maximum in the top consumer - us.
Ozone
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 13,
“Ozone (O3) is a molecule formed by three atoms of oxygen.”
What it means: A molecule of three oxygen atoms; high in the atmosphere it forms a shield against the Sun's harmful UV radiation, though near the ground it is a deadly poison.

Must-know concepts AUTHORED FRAMING

…and where they’re tested

1
Ecosystem and its components
Every ecosystem has biotic components (all the living organisms) and abiotic components (physical factors like temperature, rainfall, wind, soil and minerals). The living organisms group by how they feed: producers (green plants, some bacteria) make food by photosynthesis, consumers (herbivores, carnivores, omnivores, parasites) eat others, and decomposers (bacteria, fungi) break down the dead into simple substances the plants reuse. Natural ecosystems are forests, ponds and lakes; gardens and crop-fields are human-made.
3-mark: list the biotic and abiotic components of an ecosystem; role of decomposers in the ecosystem.
2
Food chains, food webs and trophic levels
A food chain is a series of organisms feeding on one another; each feeding step is a trophic level, with producers at the first level, primary consumers (herbivores) at the second, secondary consumers at the third and tertiary consumers at the fourth. In nature an organism is usually eaten by several kinds of organisms, so the many chains interconnect into a branching food web rather than a single straight line.
2-3 mark: identify a valid food chain and name its trophic levels; difference between a food chain and a food web.
3
Flow of energy and the 10% law
Energy flow is one-way: green plants in a terrestrial ecosystem capture only about 1% of the sunlight falling on their leaves and store it as food energy. When that food is eaten, an average of only 10% of the food is built into the consumer's own body and passed on - the rest is lost as heat, digestion and work. Because so little energy reaches the next level, food chains generally have only three or four steps, and the energy never returns to a lower level.
3-mark: state the 10% law and explain why food chains are limited to three or four trophic levels; why energy flow is unidirectional.
4
Biological magnification
Pesticides and other chemicals sprayed on crops wash into the soil and water and are taken up by plants and aquatic plants, entering the food chain. Because these chemicals are not degradable, they are not broken down or thrown out but accumulate progressively at each higher trophic level. As human beings sit at the top of most food chains, the maximum concentration collects in us - this is biological magnification, and it cannot always be washed off our food.
3-mark: what is biological magnification and why is its level different at different trophic levels.
5
Ozone layer and its depletion
Ozone (O3) high in the atmosphere shields the Earth from the Sun's ultraviolet (UV) radiation, which causes skin cancer. It forms when high-energy UV splits molecular oxygen (O2) into free atoms that then join O2 to make O3. From the 1980s the ozone amount dropped sharply, linked to synthetic CFCs used as refrigerants and in fire extinguishers; in 1987 UNEP forged an agreement to freeze CFC production at 1986 levels.
2-3 mark: how is ozone formed and why is damage to the ozone layer a cause for concern; which chemicals cause its depletion.
6
Managing the garbage we produce
Substances broken down by biological processes (the action of bacteria and saprophytes) are biodegradable; those that are not are non-biodegradable. Enzymes are specific, so micro-organisms cannot break down man-made materials like plastics, which persist for a long time and may harm members of the ecosystem. Changes in life-style and packaging have made more of our waste non-biodegradable, so separating and disposing of waste has become a serious environmental problem.
3-mark: distinguish biodegradable and non-biodegradable substances with examples; two ways each type affects the environment.

Example Section 13.1.1 (flow of energy) walkthrough,
“Trace how energy passes along the food chain grass to deer to tiger, and explain why so little reaches the top carnivore. [authored prompt]”
1
Energy enters at the producers.Grass, a green producer, captures only about 1% of the sunlight that falls on its leaves and stores it as food energy - this is the energy that enters the food chain.
1 mark
2
First transfer to the herbivore.When the deer (primary consumer) eats the grass, an average of only 10% of the food eaten is turned into its own body; the rest is lost as heat to the environment and used in digestion and work.
1 mark
3
Second transfer to the carnivore.When the tiger (secondary consumer) eats the deer, again only about 10% carries over, so the energy available drops to roughly a tenth at each successive trophic level.
1 mark
4
Why chains stay short.Because the loss at each step is so great, very little usable energy remains after four trophic levels, so food chains generally consist of only three or four steps.

The closing point on why chains are limited to three or four steps completes the reasoning but is not separately credited in the 3-mark scheme.

Problem-solving strategies AUTHORED
→
"Which is a food chain?" MCQ?A valid chain must run producer to consumer to consumer with each eating the one before it - reject options that are all plants or all animals (e.g. grass, wheat, mango is NOT a chain; grass, goat, human IS).
→
10% numerical asked?Take a tenth at every step: if producers hold 10000 J, herbivores get about 1000 J, small carnivores about 100 J, top carnivores about 10 J - then explain the loss is why chains rarely pass four levels.
→
"Differences between" asked?Answer in a two-column table (biodegradable vs non-biodegradable, food chain vs food web) - one clear contrasting point per row scores faster than a paragraph.
→
"Why is it a concern?" add-on?Give the reason, not just the fact - ozone depletion matters because the layer shields us from the Sun's UV that causes skin cancer; biological magnification matters because we are at the top and get the most concentrated dose.
Common mistakes that cost marks

Where students lose easy marks

✕
Mixing up the 1% and the 10%: producers capture about 1% of the SUNLIGHT that falls on leaves, but only about 10% of the FOOD energy transfers from one trophic level to the next. Stating either figure for the wrong step loses the mark.
✕
Confusing a food chain with a food web - a chain is a single straight line of who-eats-whom; a web is many interconnected chains. Examiners deduct if the two terms are swapped.
✕
Reversing the direction of energy flow. Energy flow is unidirectional - it never returns from a consumer back to the producer or to a previous level.
✕
Calling all ozone harmful (or all helpful). Ozone high in the atmosphere is a protective shield; the same molecule at ground level is a poison - say WHERE the ozone is.
✕
Writing that plastics or DDT are biodegradable. Man-made plastics and non-degradable pesticides are non-biodegradable and persist for a long time - only substances broken down by biological processes are biodegradable.
Formula strip
10% law of energy transfer
About 10% of the energy at one trophic level passes to the next; the other ~90% is lost as heat, digestion and work
Energy captured by producers
Green plants capture only about 1% of the sunlight falling on their leaves and store it as food energy
Length of a food chain
Food chains generally have only 3 or 4 trophic levels - little usable energy remains after four levels
Trophic levels (order)
Producers (1st) to Primary consumers / herbivores (2nd) to Secondary consumers (3rd) to Tertiary consumers (4th)
Ozone formation
O2 --(UV)--> O + O (free oxygen atoms); then O + O2 --> O3 (ozone)
