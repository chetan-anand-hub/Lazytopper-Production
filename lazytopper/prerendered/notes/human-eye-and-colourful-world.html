The Big Idea AUTHORED

One lens focuses your world; the sky paints it.

The eye lens focuses light onto the retina, and by changing its focal length (accommodation) it lets you see both near and far. When that adjustment fails you get myopia, hypermetropia or presbyopia, each corrected by a specific spectacle lens. The 'colourful world' half is pure refraction of sunlight: a prism splits white light into VIBGYOR (dispersion), the atmosphere bends starlight and sunlight (so stars twinkle and the Sun is seen about two minutes early at sunrise), and tiny particles scatter short-wavelength light more (so the clear sky looks blue and the Sun looks red at sunrise and sunset).

Definitions the board rewards NCERT VERBATIM

State these in NCERT’s exact words

Accommodation (power of accommodation)
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 10,
“The ability of the eye lens to adjust its focal length is called accommodation.”
What it means: The ciliary muscles squeeze or relax the eye lens to change its focal length: relaxed muscles give a thin lens (long focal length, distant objects clear); contracted muscles give a thick lens (short focal length, near objects clear).
Least distance of distinct vision (near point)
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 10,
“The minimum distance, at which objects can be seen most distinctly without strain, is called the least distance of distinct vision. It is also called the near point of the eye.”
What it means: The closest an object can be while still looking sharp without strain. For a normal young adult eye it is about 25 cm.
Myopia (near-sightedness)
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 10,
“Myopia is also known as near-sightedness. A person with myopia can see nearby objects clearly but cannot see distant objects distinctly.”
What it means: The image of a distant object falls in front of the retina (eyeball too long or lens too curved). Corrected with a concave (diverging) lens that pushes the image back onto the retina.
Hypermetropia (far-sightedness)
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 10,
“Hypermetropia is also known as far-sightedness. A person with hypermetropia can see distant objects clearly but cannot see nearby objects distinctly.”
What it means: The image of a nearby object would form behind the retina (eyeball too short or lens focal length too long). Corrected with a convex (converging) lens that adds the missing focusing power.
Presbyopia
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 10,
“The power of accommodation of the eye usually decreases with ageing. … This defect is called Presbyopia.”
What it means: An age-related loss of accommodation, so the near point recedes. Often it comes with myopia too, and is corrected with bi-focal lenses (concave upper part for distance, convex lower part for reading).
Angle of deviation (through a prism)
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 10,
“The peculiar shape of the prism makes the emergent ray bend at an angle to the direction of the incident ray. This angle is called the angle of deviation.”
What it means: How much the prism turns the light from its original path. Because a prism's faces are inclined (not parallel like a slab), the emergent ray does not stay parallel to the incident ray.
Dispersion of light
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 10,
“The splitting of light into its component colours is called dispersion.”
What it means: Different colours bend by different amounts in a prism (red the least, violet the most), so white light fans out into its VIBGYOR components.

Must-know concepts AUTHORED FRAMING

…and where they’re tested

1
The human eye and its parts
Light enters through the cornea (where most refraction happens), the iris controls the pupil size and hence how much light gets in, and the crystalline lens fine-tunes the focus to form a real, inverted image on the light-sensitive retina. The ciliary muscles change the lens's shape; the optic nerve carries the signal to the brain.
1-mark MCQ on which part does what (cornea / retina / ciliary muscles) and label-the-diagram
2
Accommodation, near point and far point
The eye adjusts its focal length by changing the curvature of the lens (accommodation). A normal eye sees clearly from its near point (about 25 cm) out to its far point (infinity). The focal length cannot fall below a minimum, which is why a page held too close looks blurred.
1-mark 'what is power of accommodation' + stating the 25 cm near point and infinity far point
3
Defects of vision and their correction
Three common refractive defects: myopia (distant image before the retina) is corrected with a concave lens; hypermetropia (near image beyond the retina) with a convex lens; presbyopia (ageing loss of accommodation) often needs bi-focal lenses. Identify the defect, then pick the lens and draw the corrective ray diagram.
3-mark identify-the-defect + corrective-lens ray diagram + power numerical
4
Prism, dispersion and the spectrum
A prism's inclined faces deviate light and, because each colour deviates by a different amount, split white light into the VIBGYOR spectrum (red bends least, violet most). A second inverted prism can recombine the colours back into white light, which is how Newton proved sunlight is made of seven colours. A rainbow is nature's spectrum from water droplets acting as tiny prisms.
2-3 mark dispersion / VIBGYOR reasoning and 'why does a prism disperse but a slab does not'
5
Atmospheric refraction and scattering of light
Atmospheric refraction (light bending through air of changing density) explains why stars twinkle but planets do not, and why the Sun is seen about 2 minutes before actual sunrise and 2 minutes after actual sunset. Scattering explains colour: fine particles scatter shorter (blue) wavelengths more, so the clear sky is blue, while near the horizon the long path scatters away the blue and the Sun looks red at sunrise and sunset.
2-3 mark 'why do stars twinkle / why is the sky blue / why is the Sun red at sunrise' reasoning

Ch 10,
“Make a diagram to show how hypermetropia is corrected. The near point of a hypermetropic eye is 1 m. What is the power of the lens required to correct this defect? Assume that the near point of the normal eye is 25 cm.”
Problem-solving strategies AUTHORED
→
Defect question?Name the defect, then map it: myopia goes to a concave lens, hypermetropia to a convex lens, presbyopia to bi-focal. Always add the corrective ray diagram if the marks allow.
→
Corrective-lens numerical?Myopia: object at infinity, image at the far point, f = -(far point in metres). Hypermetropia: object at 25 cm, image at the eye's near point. Then P = 1/f with f in metres.
→
Why does the sky look blue / Sun red?Both are scattering. Fine particles scatter short (blue) wavelengths most, so the sky is blue; at sunrise/sunset the long air path scatters the blue away and mostly red reaches you.
→
Why do stars twinkle but planets do not?Stars are point sources, so atmospheric refraction makes their tiny light flicker; planets are extended sources whose many points average out, so they do not twinkle.
Common mistakes that cost marks

Where students lose easy marks

✕
Swapping the corrective lenses — myopia needs a concave (diverging) lens, hypermetropia needs a convex (converging) lens. Mixing them up loses the whole mark.
✕
Forgetting the sign / units in the power numerical — f must be in metres and a diverging lens gives a negative power (e.g. -1.25 D), not a positive one.
✕
Saying the eye lens 'moves' to focus — it does not move; the ciliary muscles change its curvature (focal length). That is accommodation.
✕
Confusing dispersion with scattering — a prism disperses white light by colour-dependent refraction; the sky is blue because of scattering by tiny particles. They are different phenomena.

This chapter is examined on rules, not derivations: how the eye adjusts focus, which lens fixes which defect, and how a prism bends light. Lock these in and every ray diagram, defect question and 'explain why' falls into place.

How the eye focuses (accommodation)
1
Ciliary muscles relaxed → lens thin → focal length LONGER → distant objects clear.
2
Ciliary muscles contracted → lens thicker → focal length SHORTER → nearby objects clear.
3
Near point of a normal eye ≈ 25 cm; far point = infinity.
4
Focal length cannot drop below a minimum — so an object held closer than 25 cm looks blurred.

Defect → corrective lens (sign of power)

  • −Myopia (near-sightedness): concave (diverging) lens of suitable power — negative power.
  • +Hypermetropia (far-sightedness): convex (converging) lens of appropriate power — positive power.
  • ±Presbyopia: bi-focal lenses — concave upper part for distant vision, convex lower part for near vision.
Refraction of light through a prism
1
At the first face (air → glass) the ray bends TOWARDS the normal.
2
At the second face (glass → air) the ray bends AWAY from the normal.
3
The emergent ray is turned from the incident direction by the angle of deviation (∠D).
4
Unlike a parallel-sided slab, a prism's inclined faces mean the emergent ray is NOT parallel to the incident ray — and different colours deviate by different amounts (dispersion).

3 competency questions from the CBSE board pattern, with the full step-marked solution for each — the marks are shown per step, as the official scheme awards them.

5 marksSection D

Savera passed a beam of white light through a series of equilateral prisms (Prism 1, 2, 3). (a) What colour(s) will be seen on the screen? (b) Copy the diagram and draw the beam entering Prism 1 and emerging from Prism 3 and falling on the screen. (c) Name all the processes that take place when the beam of light enters Prism 1 and emerges from Prism 3.

Step-marked solution
  1. [1 mark] (a) Prism 1 disperses the white light into its seven constituent colours VIBGYOR (violet, indigo, blue, green, yellow, orange, red).
  2. [1 mark] (a) Prisms 2 and 3 (suitably inverted) recombine these colours, so the beam finally falling on the screen appears WHITE again (Newton's recombination experiment).
  3. [1 mark] (b) Diagram: the white beam enters Prism 1 and splits into the VIBGYOR spectrum.
  4. [1 mark] (b) Diagram: the spectrum passes through Prisms 2 and 3 and recombines into a single white beam falling on the screen.
  5. [1 mark] (c) Processes taking place: dispersion (splitting of white light), refraction (bending at each prism surface) and recombination of the colours.
5 marksSection D

(a) Rupal suffers from myopia. Where would the image form in her eye? (b) Name the type of lens that is generally used to correct myopia. (c) Rupal underwent cataract surgery and her eye lens was replaced with an artificial lens with a fixed focal length, made of a plastic material, silicone. State one likely visual disadvantage that Rupal is likely to experience as compared to a person who has normal eyesight. (d) Identify the parts of the eye labeled (i)-(iv) in the diagram: (i) changes focal length of lens; (ii) causes most refraction; (iii) controls amount of light entering; (iv) acts as screen.

Step-marked solution
  1. [1 mark] (a) In myopia (short-sightedness), the image of a distant object forms in FRONT of the retina (eye lens too convergent / eyeball too long).
  2. [1 mark] (b) A concave (diverging) lens of suitable negative power is used to correct myopia, shifting the image back onto the retina.
  3. [1 mark] (c) With a fixed-focal-length artificial lens she loses the power of accommodation — the lens cannot change shape to focus on both near and distant objects (unlike the natural lens controlled by ciliary muscles).
  4. [1 mark] (d) (i) ciliary muscle — changes the focal length of the eye lens; (ii) cornea — causes most (~80%) of the refraction.
  5. [1 mark] (d) (iii) iris/pupil — controls the amount of light entering the eye; (iv) retina — acts as the light-sensitive screen.
5 marksSection D

A person is unable to see objects distinctly placed within 75 cm from his eyes. (a) Name the defect of vision the person is suffering from. (b) List its two possible causes. (c) Calculate the power of the lens needed to correct this defect. Assume that the near point for the normal eye is 25 cm.

Step-marked solution
  1. [1 mark] (a) The defect is HYPERMETROPIA (long-sightedness) — the near point has moved away from the normal 25 cm.
  2. [1 mark] (b) Cause 1: the curvature of the eye lens decreases (eye lens becomes less convergent, focal length too long).
  3. [1 mark] (b) Cause 2: the eyeball becomes too short, so the image of a near object forms behind the retina.
  4. [1 mark] (c) To correct, an object at 25 cm must give a virtual image at the near point 75 cm: u = −25 cm, v = −75 cm. Lens formula 1/f = 1/v − 1/u = 1/(−75) − 1/(−25) = −1/75 + 3/75 = 2/75 ⟹ f = 37.5 cm = 0.375 m.
  5. [1 mark] (c) Power P = 1/f(in m) = 1/0.375 ≈ +2.67 D (positive ⟹ convex lens).
