Probability

★

What the board actually asks: single-event probability from a bag, die, coin or a deck of cards, the complement rule P(not E) = 1 - P(E), and the impossible-0 / sure-1 / 0 to 1 range facts - together these carry almost every mark in this chapter.

The Big Idea AUTHORED

Count the wanted, count the total, divide.

Probability measures how likely an event is, on a scale from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain). When every outcome of an experiment is equally likely, the theoretical (classical) probability of an event E is just P(E)=all possible outcomesfavourable outcomes​. The whole chapter is that one idea applied to coins, dice, cards and bags of marbles - plus one shortcut, the complement P(not E)=1−P(E), and one sanity check, 0≤P(E)≤1.

Definitions the board rewards NCERT VERBATIM

State these in NCERT’s exact words

Theoretical (classical) probability
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 14,
“The theoretical probability (also called classical probability) of an event E, written as P(E), is defined as P(E) = Number of outcomes favourable to E / Number of all possible outcomes of the experiment, where we assume that the outcomes of the experiment are equally likely.”
What it means: P(E)=all possible outcomesoutcomes favourable to E​. It ONLY applies when the outcomes are equally likely - check that first, or the formula is invalid.
Elementary event
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 14,
“An event having only one outcome of the experiment is called an elementary event.”
What it means: an event with exactly ONE outcome (e.g. 'the die shows 5'). Across an experiment, the probabilities of all elementary events add up to 1.
Complement / complementary events
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 14,
“The event E̅, representing 'not E', is called the complement of the event E. We also say that E and E̅ are complementary events.”
What it means: 'not E' is everything in the sample space outside E. Because E and E together fill the whole space, P(E)=1−P(E) - the workhorse shortcut for 'not' and 'at least one' questions.
Impossible event
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 14,
“the probability of an event which is impossible to occur is 0. Such an event is called an impossible event.”
What it means: an event with NO favourable outcome (e.g. getting an 8 on an ordinary die). Its probability is exactly 0.
Sure (certain) event
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 14,
“the probability of an event which is sure (or certain) to occur is 1. Such an event is called a sure event or a certain event.”
What it means: an event whose favourable outcomes are ALL the outcomes (e.g. getting a number less than 7 on a die). Its probability is exactly 1.
📖Key terms — NCERT verbatim (state word-for-word)
Equally likely outcomes
We refer to this by saying that the outcomes head and tail, are equally likely. (Ch 14, )
Sum of elementary events = 1
the sum of the probabilities of all the elementary events of an experiment is 1. (Ch 14, )
Range of P(E)
the numerator (number of outcomes favourable to the event E) is always less than or equal to the denominator (the number of all possible outcomes). (Ch 14, )
Must-know concepts AUTHORED FRAMING

…and where they’re tested

1
Classical probability = favourable / total
List every outcome of the experiment, confirm they are equally likely, count the favourable ones, and divide: P(E)=totalfavourable​. Always simplify the fraction.
1-mark MCQ + 2-3 mark 'find the probability' (bag / die / coin / cards)
2
Sample space and elementary events
The sample space is the set of all possible outcomes. An elementary event has just one outcome. A useful check: the probabilities of all the elementary events of an experiment add to 1.
listing outcomes for one/two coins or dice before counting
3
Complementary events - the shortcut
E and 'not E' together cover the whole sample space, so P(not E)=1−P(E). When a question says 'not', 'at least one', or gives you the opposite event, the complement is usually the fast route.
'not an ace', 'at least one head', 'not a 5' - complement saves counting
4
Impossible (0), sure (1), and the range
An impossible event has probability 0; a sure event has probability 1; every probability lies in between: 0≤P(E)≤1. A value below 0 or above 1 can never be a probability.
'which of these cannot be a probability?' MCQ (e.g. -1.5, 15%, 0.7)
5
Two-dice / two-coin sample spaces
For two dice, list ordered pairs - order matters, so (1,4)=(4,1) - giving 6×6=36 equally likely outcomes. Then count the pairs favourable to the event. Two coins give {(H,H),(H,T),(T,H),(T,T)}.
'sum on two dice' (NCERT Example 13) - a Section C/D fixture

Common mistakes that cost marks

Where students lose easy marks

✕
Applying the formula when outcomes are not equally likely - e.g. 'the car starts or does not start'. The classical formula only holds for equally likely outcomes.
✕
Treating two-dice outcomes as unordered - (1,4) and (4,1) are different, so the sample space has 36 pairs, not 21.
✕
Not writing 0 for an impossible event or 1 for a sure event - these are exact numbers, not 'no answer'.
✕
Leaving the fraction unsimplified, or giving a probability above 1 - always reduce, and sanity-check that 0≤P(E)≤1.

Why 0 <= P(E) <= 1, and why P(E) + P(not E) = 1

These two facts are not memorised rules - they fall straight out of the definition P(E) = favourable / total. NCERT derives them by reasoning about the favourable count against the total count (pp.206-207). Understanding the argument means you never doubt a bound or a complement in the exam.

1
Start from the definition of probability.
P(E)=all possible outcomesfavourable outcomes​
The classical definition, valid because the outcomes are equally likely (p.203).
2
The favourable count is never negative, and the total count is positive, so P(E) is never below 0.
P(E)≥0
The least it can be is 0, when NO outcome is favourable - an impossible event, e.g. getting an 8 on a die: 0/6 = 0 (pp.206-207).
3
The favourable outcomes are part of all the outcomes, so their count is at most the total; hence P(E) is never above 1.
P(E)≤1
NCERT: the numerator is always less than or equal to the denominator. The most it can be is 1, when EVERY outcome is favourable - a sure event, e.g. a number less than 7 on a die: 6/6 = 1 (p.207).
4
Combining the two bounds gives the range of any probability.
0≤P(E)≤1
Stated in NCERT immediately after the numerator/denominator argument (p.207).
5
Now the complement: 'not E' is exactly the outcomes NOT favourable to E, so the two favourable counts add up to the total.
(favourable to E)+(favourable to not E)=total
E and 'not E' together cover the whole sample space with no overlap (p.206).
6
Divide through by the total count to get the complement rule.
P(E)+P(E)=1⇒P(E)=1−P(E)
NCERT: P(E) + P(not E) = 1, which gives P(not E) = 1 - P(E) (p.206).

3 competency questions from the CBSE board pattern, with the full step-marked solution for each — the marks are shown per step, as the official scheme awards them.

2 marksSection B

Diwali Fest is an annual South Asian arts & culture festival produced by the Diwali Celebration Society. In the Diwali fest, a game is played with a fair spinner (shown). The numbers on the spinner are 2, 5, 7, 9, 12, 16. Sometimes the owner will invite a player who does not win with the spinner to throw the dice as a free bonus. What is the probability that a player will get a special prize because the spinner stops on a perfect square? Show your working.

Step-marked solution
  1. [1 mark] Identify perfect squares among 2, 5, 7, 9, 12, 16: these are 9 and 16 → 2 favourable out of 6.
  2. [1 mark] P(perfect square) = 2/6 = 1/3 (or 0.33).
2 marksSection B

In the Diwali fest game, if the player gets a chance to throw a dice (as a free bonus after not winning with the fair spinner), what is the probability of getting a multiple of 2 on the dice? Show your working.

Step-marked solution
  1. [1 mark] Identify outcomes that are multiples of 2 on a die: 2, 4 and 6 → 3 favourable out of 6.
  2. [1 mark] P(multiple of 2) = 3/6 = 1/2 (or 0.5).
5 marksSection D

In a game the entry fee is ₹5. The game consists of tossing a coin 3 times. If one or two heads show, Sweta gets her entry fee back; if she throws 3 heads she gets double the entry fee; otherwise she loses. For tossing a coin three times, find the probability that she (i) loses the entry fee, (ii) gets double the entry fee, (iii) just gets her entry fee back.

Step-marked solution
  1. [1 mark] The coin is tossed 3 times, so the sample space has 2³ = 8 equally likely outcomes: HHH, HHT, HTH, THH, HTT, THT, TTH, TTT.
  2. [1 mark] (i) She loses only when no head shows (0 heads) — outcome TTT, i.e. 1 favourable outcome.
  3. [1 mark] (i) P(loses the entry fee) = 1/8.
  4. [1 mark] (ii) She gets double the fee only on 3 heads (HHH) — 1 outcome, so P(gets double) = 1/8.
  5. [1 mark] (iii) She gets her fee back on exactly 1 or 2 heads = 8 − 1(HHH) − 1(TTT) = 6 outcomes, so P = 6/8 = 3/4.
