Chemical Reactions and Equations

★

What the board actually asks: balance a skeletal equation and add the physical-state symbols (s)/(l)/(g)/(aq); identify the reaction type (combination, decomposition, displacement, double displacement/precipitation, or oxidation-reduction); classify a decomposition by its energy source (heat, light or electricity); say which substance is oxidised and which is reduced; and explain the everyday effects of oxidation - corrosion and rancidity. The balanced equation IS the answer in most questions here.

The Big Idea AUTHORED

New substances form - and we record them as a balanced equation.

A chemical reaction makes one or more new substances; you notice it as a change in state, colour or temperature, or a gas being given off. We write the change as a chemical equation and then balance it, because atoms are only rearranged, never created or destroyed. Almost every reaction in this chapter sorts into one of five types, and many are also redox reactions - oxygen or hydrogen changing hands. Most marks sit in writing the correct balanced equation with the right classification.

Definitions the board rewards NCERT VERBATIM

State these in NCERT’s exact words

Balanced chemical equation
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 1,
“A chemical equation is balanced so that the numbers of atoms of each type involved in a chemical reaction are the same on the reactant and product sides of the equation.”
What it means: The same number of atoms of every element on the left and the right of the arrow - it must obey the law of conservation of mass.
Combination reaction
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 1,
“Such a reaction in which a single product is formed from two or more reactants is known as a combination reaction.”
What it means: Two or more reactants join to give a single product, e.g. calcium oxide + water gives slaked lime.
Decomposition reaction
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 1,
“a single reactant breaks down to give simpler products. This is a decomposition reaction.”
What it means: The opposite of combination - one reactant breaks down into two or more simpler products, usually needing heat, light or electricity.
Displacement reaction
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 1,
“When an element displaces another element from its compound, a displacement reaction occurs.”
What it means: A more reactive element pushes a less reactive one out of its compound - iron displaces copper from copper sulphate solution.
Double displacement reaction
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 1,
“Such reactions in which there is an exchange of ions between the reactants are called double displacement reactions.”
What it means: The two reactants swap their ions/partners; an insoluble product often drops out as a precipitate.
Oxidation and reduction
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 1,
“if a substance gains oxygen or loses hydrogen during a reaction, it is oxidised. If a substance loses oxygen or gains hydrogen during a reaction, it is reduced.”
What it means: Oxidation adds oxygen (or removes hydrogen); reduction removes oxygen (or adds hydrogen). When one substance is oxidised and the other reduced in the same reaction it is a redox reaction.
Redox reaction (oxidation-reduction)
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 1,
“one reactant gets oxidised while the other gets reduced during a reaction. Such reactions are called oxidation-reduction reactions or redox reactions.”
What it means: A reaction in which one substance is oxidised and, at the same time, another substance is reduced.
Exothermic reaction
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 1,
“Reactions in which heat is released along with the formation of products are called exothermic chemical reactions.”
What it means: A reaction that gives out heat to the surroundings - respiration, combustion, and quicklime added to water are examples.
Endothermic reaction
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 1,
“Reactions in which energy is absorbed are known as endothermic reactions.”
What it means: A reaction that takes in energy from the surroundings - most decomposition reactions are endothermic.
Corrosion
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 1,
“When a metal is attacked by substances around it such as moisture, acids, etc., it is said to corrode and this process is called corrosion.”
What it means: The slow eating-away of a metal by air, moisture or acids - the rusting of iron is the everyday example.
Rancidity
📖NCERT verbatim — memorise this exact wording· Ch 1,
“When fats and oils are oxidised, they become rancid and their smell and taste change.”
What it means: The oxidation of fats and oils in food, which spoils their smell and taste; slowed by antioxidants or by flushing the pack with nitrogen.

Must-know concepts AUTHORED FRAMING

…and where they’re tested

1
Writing and balancing an equation
Write the reactants on the left and products on the right of an arrow, using chemical formulae. Balance by the hit-and-trial method - start with the compound that has the most atoms and change only the coefficients, never the formula itself. Finally add state symbols (s), (l), (g), (aq) to make the equation informative.
3-mark: balance a skeletal equation and write it with correct state symbols.
2
Combination reactions
Two or more reactants combine to form a single product, for example calcium oxide + water gives slaked lime, or magnesium burns in air to give magnesium oxide. Combination reactions are usually exothermic - heat is given out.
identify a combination reaction, write the equation, and state whether it is exothermic.
3
Decomposition reactions
A single reactant breaks down into two or more simpler products. The energy needed is supplied as heat (thermal, e.g. limestone to quicklime), light (photolytic, e.g. silver chloride in sunlight), or electricity (electrolytic, e.g. electrolysis of water). Decomposition reactions are usually endothermic.
classify a decomposition by its energy source (heat/light/electricity) and write one equation for each.
4
Displacement reactions
A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from its salt solution, for example iron displaces copper from copper sulphate solution. The iron nail turns brownish and the blue colour of the copper sulphate solution fades.
reactivity comparison plus the colour changes observed with the iron nail and copper sulphate.
5
Double displacement and precipitation
The reactants exchange ions; when an insoluble salt forms it drops out as a precipitate, for example sodium sulphate + barium chloride gives a white precipitate of barium sulphate. Any reaction that produces a precipitate is a precipitation reaction.
name the precipitate and write the balanced equation with state symbols.
6
Oxidation-reduction (redox)
In a redox reaction one substance is oxidised (gains oxygen or loses hydrogen) while the other is reduced (loses oxygen or gains hydrogen) at the same time. For example, when hydrogen is passed over heated copper(II) oxide, CuO is reduced to copper and hydrogen is oxidised to water.
state which substance is oxidised and which is reduced, with the reason.
7
Effects of oxidation in everyday life
Corrosion is the slow attack of a metal by moisture and acids in its surroundings - the rusting of iron is the commonest example, and it damages railings, bridges, ships and car bodies. Rancidity is the oxidation of fats and oils in food, which changes their smell and taste; it is slowed by adding antioxidants, using air-tight packs, or flushing the pack with nitrogen.
2-mark: define corrosion and rancidity with an example each; why food packs are flushed with nitrogen.

Ch 1, Example Balancing worked example (Steps I-VII, Eq. 1.10),
“[authored prompt] Balance the skeletal equation Fe + H2O -> Fe3O4 + H2 by the hit-and-trial method and write it with physical states.”
1
Count the atoms.LHS: Fe 1, H 2, O 1. RHS: Fe 3, H 2, O 4 - the skeletal equation is unbalanced.
½ mark
2
Start with the biggest formula.Fe3O4 has 4 oxygen atoms, so balance oxygen by putting 4 before H2O.
Fe + 4H2O -> Fe3O4 + H2
Concept: start with the most atoms
½ mark
3
Balance hydrogen.There are now 8 H atoms on the left, so put 4 before H2 on the right.
Fe + 4H2O -> Fe3O4 + 4H2
½ mark
4
Balance iron.There are 3 Fe atoms on the right, so put 3 before Fe on the left.
3Fe + 4H2O -> Fe3O4 + 4H2
½ mark
5
Add physical states.Water is used here as steam, so the complete balanced equation is:
3Fe(s) + 4H2O(g) -> Fe3O4(s) + 4H2(g)
1 mark
Mark-scheme logic (authored): CBSE 3-mark: the correct coefficients reached stage by stage (2 - about half a mark per balanced element/step) plus a fully balanced equation carrying correct state symbols (1). Never change a formula - only the coefficients.

Problem-solving strategies AUTHORED
→
Balance first.Draw a box around each formula and change only the coefficients, never the subscripts. Start with the compound that has the most atoms, then work through the other elements.
→
Which type is it?Count reactants and products - one product means combination; one reactant means decomposition; a single element swapped out means displacement; ions exchanged between two compounds means double displacement.
→
Redox?Track the oxygen (or hydrogen): whichever substance gains oxygen or loses hydrogen is oxidised; the other is reduced.
→
Complete equation asked?Add the state symbols (s), (l), (g), (aq) and write the condition - Heat, Sunlight or Electricity - above the arrow when the question wants a complete equation.
Common mistakes that cost marks

Where students lose easy marks

✕
Changing a formula to balance an equation - e.g. writing H2O4 instead of putting a 4 before H2O. Only the coefficients may be changed, never the subscripts inside a formula.
✕
Leaving out the state symbols (s)/(l)/(g)/(aq) when the question asks for a complete or a balanced-with-states equation.
✕
Confusing displacement (a single element is displaced) with double displacement (ions are exchanged between two compounds).
✕
Reversing oxidation and reduction. Remember it by the oxygen: gain of oxygen is oxidation, loss of oxygen is reduction.
✕
Calling a precipitation reaction a combination reaction. A white insoluble product means a double displacement (precipitation) reaction, not a combination.
Formula strip
Combination
A + B -> AB (two or more reactants -> a single product)
Decomposition
AB -> A + B (one reactant -> two or more products)
Displacement
A + BC -> AC + B (A more reactive than B)
Double displacement
AB + CD -> AD + CB (ions exchanged)
Oxidation / Reduction
Oxidation: gain O or lose H . Reduction: lose O or gain H
Conservation of mass
atoms of each element (reactants) = atoms of each element (products)
Everyday oxidation
Corrosion (rusting of iron) . Rancidity (fats/oils oxidise) - slowed by antioxidants / nitrogen flush

3 competency questions from the CBSE board pattern, with the full step-marked solution for each — the marks are shown per step, as the official scheme awards them.

1 markSection A

A single displacement reaction is represented below: PQ + R → PR + Q. Which of the following is true about the reactants and products? Option A: R = cation in product, PR more stable than PQ. Option B: R = cation in product, PR less stable. Option C: R = anion in product, PR more stable. Option D: R = anion in product, PR less stable.

Step-marked solution
  1. [0.5 mark] Single displacement: R displaces Q from PQ ⟹ R bonds with P. For R to displace Q, R must form a more stable compound (PR) than PQ.
  2. [0.5 mark] R in original was free element; after reaction in PR, P is the cation (metal) and R becomes the anion (gained electrons from displacement). So R = anion, PR more stable.
1 markSection A

Which of the following observations is correct for the experimental diagram in which copper metal is heated strongly in air?

Step-marked solution
  1. [0.5 mark] When copper is heated strongly in air, it combines with atmospheric oxygen — this is an oxidation reaction: 2 Cu + O2 → 2 CuO.
  2. [0.5 mark] The product CuO (copper(II) oxide) is BLACK in colour. Reddish-brown copper is converted to black CuO; the metal undergoes oxidation (gain of oxygen).
5 marksSection D

An iron nail is placed in each of three test tubes: (A) tap water, (B) boiled distilled water covered with a layer of oil, (C) anhydrous calcium chloride with dry air. After a week only the nail in tube A has rusted. (i) What is rusting, chemically? (ii) Explain the result in tube B. (iii) Explain the result in tube C. (iv) What TWO conditions does this experiment prove are necessary for rusting? (v) Suggest one method to protect the nail.

Step-marked solution
  1. [1 mark] Rusting is the slow oxidation of iron in moist air to form hydrated iron(III) oxide (rust), 4Fe + 3O2 + xH2O -> 2Fe2O3.xH2O.
  2. [1 mark] Tube B: boiling removes dissolved air (oxygen) and the oil layer keeps fresh air out, so no oxygen is available and the nail does not rust.
  3. [1 mark] Tube C: anhydrous calcium chloride absorbs moisture, so the air is dry; with no water the nail does not rust.
  4. [1 mark] The experiment proves that BOTH air (oxygen) and moisture (water) are necessary for rusting; only tube A had both.
  5. [1 mark] Protection: coat the nail — e.g. galvanising (zinc coat), painting, oiling or greasing — to keep out air and moisture.
