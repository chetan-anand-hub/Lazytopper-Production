Metals and Non-metals — Class 10 Science, NCERT Chapter 3

Board weightage: high · 12 step-marked questions · 33 marks in total

Metals give up electrons - and that one habit explains all their chemistry. Metals are lustrous, malleable, ductile conductors that tend to LOSE electrons and form positive ions, so they react with oxygen, water and acids and can push a less reactive metal out of its salt. Ordering metals by how readily they react gives the reactivity (activity) series, and that single list then tells us how each metal occurs in nature and how it must be extracted and refined. Non-metals behave in the opposite way - they gain electrons - so a metal and a non-metal together form an ionic compound. Most board marks here come from writing the correct balanced equation and reasoning with the activity series.

Formula sheet

  • Metal + Oxygen Metal + Oxygen -> Metal oxide (mostly basic; Al2O3 and ZnO are amphoteric)
  • Metal + Water Metal + Water -> Metal oxide + Hydrogen . Metal oxide + Water -> Metal hydroxide
  • Metal + Acid Metal + Dilute acid -> Salt + Hydrogen
  • Displacement Metal A + Salt solution of B -> Salt solution of A + Metal B (A more reactive than B)
  • Activity series K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Pb > [H] > Cu > Hg > Ag > Au
  • Roasting / Calcination Roasting: sulphide ore + excess air -> oxide . Calcination: carbonate ore + limited air -> oxide
  • Thermit reaction Fe2O3 + 2Al -> 2Fe + Al2O3 + Heat (aluminium reduces iron(III) oxide)

Key definitions

Metallic lustre
The natural shine of a clean metal surface - which is why freshly rubbed iron or copper looks bright. NCERT 2026-27 Ch3 p.37
Malleability
The ability to be hammered into thin sheets without breaking - gold and silver are the most malleable metals. NCERT 2026-27 Ch3 p.38
Ductility
The ability to be pulled out into thin wires - gold is the most ductile metal. NCERT 2026-27 Ch3 p.38
Amphoteric oxides
Oxides that behave both as a base (with acids) and as an acid (with bases) - aluminium oxide and zinc oxide are the standard examples. NCERT 2026-27 Ch3 p.41
Reactivity (activity) series
The ranking K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Pb > [H] > Cu > Hg > Ag > Au - a more reactive metal displaces a less reactive one. NCERT 2026-27 Ch3 p.45
Ionic (electrovalent) compounds
Compounds like NaCl or MgCl2 made when a metal donates electrons to a non-metal, giving oppositely charged ions held by strong electrostatic attraction. NCERT 2026-27 Ch3 p.48
Minerals
Naturally occurring elements or compounds in the earth's crust that contain a metal. NCERT 2026-27 Ch3 p.49
Ores
The minerals from which a metal can be extracted profitably - every ore is a mineral, but not every mineral is rich enough to be an ore. NCERT 2026-27 Ch3 p.49
Metallurgy
The whole process of getting a pure, usable metal from its ore - enrichment, then reduction, then refining. NCERT 2026-27 Ch3 p.55
Corrosion
The slow eating-away of a metal surface by air and moisture - the rusting of iron is the everyday example, and it needs BOTH air and water. NCERT 2026-27 Ch3 p.55
Alloy
A uniform mixture made by melting a base metal and dissolving other elements in it - e.g. steel (iron + carbon), brass, bronze, solder. NCERT 2026-27 Ch3 p.54
Sonorous
The metals that produce a sound on striking a hard surface are said to be sonorous. NCERT 2026-27 Ch3 p.39
Allotrope
Carbon is a non-metal that can exist in different forms. Each form is called an allotrope. NCERT 2026-27 Ch3 p.40
Gangue
Ores mined from the earth are usually contaminated with large amounts of impurities such as soil, sand, etc., called gangue. NCERT 2026-27 Ch3 p.50
Roasting
The sulphide ores are converted into oxides by heating strongly in the presence of excess air. This process is known as roasting. NCERT 2026-27 Ch3 p.51
Calcination
The carbonate ores are changed into oxides by heating strongly in limited air. This process is known as calcination. NCERT 2026-27 Ch3 p.51
Amalgam
If one of the metals is mercury, then the alloy is known as an amalgam. NCERT 2026-27 Ch3 p.54

What the board actually asks

Distinguish metals from non-metals by their physical properties (lustre, malleability, ductility, conductivity, sonority) and their chemical behaviour; write and balance the equations for metals reacting with oxygen, water, dilute acids and salt solutions; use the reactivity (activity) series to predict displacement and to choose the right extraction method; explain how metals occur as minerals and ores and how they are enriched, reduced (by carbon or electrolysis) and refined; show how an ionic (electrovalent) compound forms by the transfer of electrons and list its properties; and explain corrosion, its prevention and alloys. The balanced equation and activity-series reasoning carry most of the marks here.

Q1. 1 mark Chemical Properties

The elements whose oxides can turn a colourless phenolphthalein solution pink are:

  1. Na and K
  2. K and C
  3. Na and S
  4. K and P

Step-marked solution

  1. [1 mark] Na and K are metals; their oxides (Na₂O, K₂O) are basic and dissolve in water to give alkalis (NaOH, KOH), which turn phenolphthalein pink — so the answer is Na and K.

Answer. Na and K

Q2. 2 marks Chemical Properties

Explain why aluminium reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid only slowly in the beginning and then more vigorously.

Step-marked solution

  1. [1 mark] Aluminium has a thin protective oxide layer (Al₂O₃) on its surface; this passive layer resists the acid, so the reaction is slow to begin with.
  2. [1 mark] Once the dilute HCl dissolves the oxide layer, the exposed aluminium reacts readily: 2Al + 6HCl → 2AlCl₃ + 3H₂, so the reaction becomes vigorous.

Answer. A protective Al₂O₃ layer slows it at first; once dissolved, fresh Al reacts fast.

Q3. 3 marks Reactivity Series

Give reasons for the following: (a) Hydrogen, though a non-metal, has been assigned a place in the reactivity (activity) series of metals. (b) A galvanised iron article remains protected against rusting even after its zinc layer is broken. (c) Carbonate and sulphide ores are usually converted into oxides before reduction during the extraction of a metal.

Step-marked solution

  1. [1 mark] (a) Hydrogen forms H⁺ ions just like metals, so it is placed in the activity series as a reference point — metals above it liberate H₂ from acids, metals below it do not.
  2. [1 mark] (b) Zinc is more reactive than iron; it acts as a sacrificial coating and corrodes in preference to iron even where the layer is broken, so the iron stays protected.
  3. [1 mark] (c) Oxides are more easily reduced to the metal than carbonates or sulphides, so ores are first converted to the oxide (by calcination or roasting) before reduction.

Answer. (a) H forms H⁺ like metals; (b) sacrificial protection by more-reactive Zn; (c) oxides reduce more easily than carbonates/sulphides.

Q4. 4 marks Extraction of Metals

Aluminium is extracted from purified bauxite (Al2O3). Instead of heating the oxide with coke, the molten oxide is decomposed by passing electricity through it. (i) Name the ore of aluminium. (ii) Why can carbon NOT be used to reduce aluminium oxide? (iii) Write the reaction occurring at the cathode. (iv) Name the substance added to alumina to lower its high melting point.

Step-marked solution

  1. [1 mark] (i) The ore is BAUXITE (Al2O3).
  2. [1 mark] (ii) Aluminium is HIGH in the reactivity series; carbon cannot reduce its oxide, so electrolytic reduction is used.
  3. [1 mark] (iii) At the cathode: Al3+ + 3e- -> Al(l).
  4. [1 mark] (iv) Cryolite (Na3AlF6) is added to lower the melting point of alumina and improve conductivity.

Answer. (i) Bauxite (ii) Al too reactive for carbon (iii) Al3+ +3e- ->Al (iv) cryolite.

Q5. 5 marks Physical Properties

Compare metals and non-metals on the basis of their PHYSICAL properties. (i) Tabulate any four contrasting physical properties with one example each. (ii) State one exception of a metal and one of a non-metal to the general trend. (iii) Explain why metals are generally good conductors of electricity.

Step-marked solution

  1. [1 mark] Lustre & state: metals are lustrous solids (e.g. silver, Ag) — mercury (Hg) is the liquid exception; non-metals are dull and are often gases or soft solids (e.g. sulphur, S).
  2. [1 mark] Malleability/ductility: metals are malleable and ductile (e.g. gold, Au, is drawn into wire); non-metals are brittle in the solid state and break on hammering (e.g. sulphur crumbles).
  3. [1 mark] Conductivity & melting point: metals conduct heat/electricity and have high melting points (e.g. copper, Cu); non-metals are insulators with low melting points (e.g. sulphur).
  4. [1 mark] Sonorousness & exceptions: metals are sonorous (ring when struck), non-metals are not; the key exceptions are mercury (a liquid metal) and graphite (a non-metal that conducts electricity).
  5. [1 mark] Reason for conductivity: in a metal the outermost valence electrons are loosely held and become free/mobile; these delocalised electrons drift under a potential difference, so the metal conducts electricity.

Answer. Metals: lustrous, malleable, ductile, conducting, sonorous, high-MP (exception Hg liquid); non-metals: dull, brittle, insulating (exception graphite conducts). Conductivity is due to free electrons.

Q6. 1 mark Displacement Reactions

The colours of the aqueous solutions of copper sulphate (CuSO₄) and iron sulphate (FeSO₄), as observed in the laboratory, are respectively:

  1. Pale green and light blue
  2. Light blue and dark green
  3. Dark green and dark blue
  4. Dark blue and pale green

Step-marked solution

  1. [1 mark] Copper sulphate solution is dark blue and iron(II) sulphate solution is pale (light) green — so the answer is 'Dark blue and pale green'.

Answer. Dark blue and pale green

Q7. 2 marks Occurrence and Ores

Name one metal and one non-metal that are obtained on a large scale from sea water.

Step-marked solution

  1. [1 mark] Metal from sea water — Magnesium (extracted from the magnesium chloride/salts dissolved in sea water).
  2. [1 mark] Non-metal from sea water — Bromine (obtained from the bromide salts present in sea water).

Answer. Metal: Magnesium; Non-metal: Bromine.

Q8. 3 marks Non-metals and their Oxides

A yellow-coloured powder X is soluble in carbon disulphide. It burns in air with a blue flame, forming a suffocating gas that turns moist blue litmus red. (a) Identify X. (b) Write the balanced equation for its burning in air. (c) State, with reason, whether X is a metal or a non-metal.

Step-marked solution

  1. [1 mark] (a) A yellow powder, soluble in carbon disulphide, that burns to a suffocating acidic gas is sulphur — so X is sulphur (S).
  2. [1 mark] (b) Balanced equation for burning in air: S + O₂ → SO₂ (sulphur dioxide).
  3. [1 mark] (c) SO₂ is acidic (turns moist blue litmus red); since it forms an acidic oxide, X is a non-metal.

Answer. (a) Sulphur; (b) S + O₂ → SO₂; (c) non-metal (forms acidic SO₂).

Q9. 4 marks Extraction of Metals

Copper is obtained from copper glance (Cu2S). The concentrated ore is partly roasted in a limited supply of air; the roasted product is then heated further with no added reducing agent and molten copper separates out (self-reduction). (i) What type of ore is copper glance? (ii) Write the equation for roasting of Cu2S to its oxide. (iii) Write the self-reduction equation that gives copper. (iv) Name the process used finally to obtain very pure copper.

Step-marked solution

  1. [1 mark] (i) Copper glance (Cu2S) is a SULPHIDE ore.
  2. [1 mark] (ii) Partial roasting: 2Cu2S(s) + 3O2(g) --heat--> 2Cu2O(s) + 2SO2(g).
  3. [1 mark] (iii) Self-reduction: 2Cu2O(s) + Cu2S(s) --heat--> 6Cu(s) + SO2(g).
  4. [1 mark] (iv) The blister copper is purified by ELECTROLYTIC REFINING.

Answer. (i) Sulphide (ii) 2Cu2S+3O2->2Cu2O+2SO2 (iii) 2Cu2O+Cu2S->6Cu+SO2 (iv) electrolytic refining.

Q10. 5 marks Chemical Properties

Compare the CHEMICAL behaviour of metals and non-metals. (i) What is the nature of the oxide each forms? Support with one equation each. (ii) How does each behave with dilute acids? (iii) On the basis of electron transfer, explain why metals are called electropositive and non-metals electronegative.

Step-marked solution

  1. [1 mark] Metal oxide is basic: 4Na(s) + O₂(g) → 2Na₂O(s); Na₂O(s) + H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq), which turns red litmus blue.
  2. [1 mark] Non-metal oxide is acidic: C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g); CO₂(g) + H₂O(l) → H₂CO₃(aq), which turns blue litmus red.
  3. [1 mark] With dilute acid a metal displaces hydrogen: Zn(s) + 2HCl(aq) → ZnCl₂(aq) + H₂(g); a non-metal cannot donate electrons to H⁺, so it gives no H₂.
  4. [1 mark] Metals are electropositive: they lose electrons to form positive ions, e.g. Mg(s) → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻.
  5. [1 mark] Non-metals are electronegative: they gain electrons to form negative ions, e.g. Cl(g) + e⁻ → Cl⁻; this opposite electron behaviour is the basis of ionic bond formation between them.

Answer. Metals → basic oxides, displace H₂ from acids, electropositive (lose e⁻); non-metals → acidic oxides, no H₂ from acids, electronegative (gain e⁻).

Q11. 1 mark Reactivity Series

To show that zinc is a more reactive metal than copper, the correct procedure is to:

  1. Add dilute nitric acid on strips of both metals
  2. Compare the transmission of heat through zinc and copper strips
  3. Prepare a solution of zinc sulphate and suspend a copper strip in it
  4. Prepare a solution of copper sulphate and suspend a zinc strip in it

Step-marked solution

  1. [1 mark] The more reactive metal displaces the less reactive one from its salt solution; zinc suspended in copper sulphate turns the strip brown (Cu deposit) and fades the blue colour, proving Zn > Cu — so the answer is 'Prepare a solution of copper sulphate and suspend a zinc strip in it'.

Answer. Suspend a zinc strip in copper sulphate solution

Q12. 2 marks Extraction of Metals

A metal M is found in nature as its carbonate MCO₃ (calamine) and is used for galvanising iron articles. (a) Name the metal M. (b) How can metal M be obtained from its carbonate ore?

Step-marked solution

  1. [1 mark] (a) Zinc is used for galvanising iron and occurs as the carbonate calamine (ZnCO₃), so M is zinc (Zn).
  2. [1 mark] (b) Calcine the carbonate to the oxide: ZnCO₃ → ZnO + CO₂; then reduce the oxide with carbon: ZnO + C → Zn + CO.

Answer. (a) Zinc. (b) Calcine ZnCO₃ → ZnO, then reduce ZnO with carbon → Zn.

Common mistakes — where marks are lost

  • Swapping roasting and calcination. Roasting = sulphide ore + excess air; calcination = carbonate ore + limited air.
  • Saying metals form acidic oxides. Metals form BASIC oxides; aluminium oxide and zinc oxide are the amphoteric exceptions. Non-metals form acidic or neutral oxides.
  • Claiming all metals react with water. Lead, copper, silver and gold do not react with water at all, and aluminium, iron and zinc react only with steam, not with cold or hot water.
  • Reversing the electrodes. In electrolytic reduction and refining the metal is deposited at the cathode (negative electrode); chlorine is liberated at the anode (positive electrode), where the impure metal dissolves.
  • Expecting hydrogen gas when a metal reacts with nitric acid. HNO3 is a strong oxidising agent, so it usually oxidises the hydrogen to water and no H2 is evolved (very dilute HNO3 with Mg or Mn is the exception).
  • Thinking ionic compounds conduct as solids. They conduct only in the molten or aqueous state, when the ions are free to move; in the solid state the ions are locked in a rigid structure.
