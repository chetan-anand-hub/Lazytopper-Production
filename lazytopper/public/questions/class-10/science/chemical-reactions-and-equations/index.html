Chemical Reactions and Equations — Class 10 Science, NCERT Chapter 1

Board weightage: high · 12 step-marked questions · 33 marks in total

New substances form - and we record them as a balanced equation. A chemical reaction makes one or more new substances; you notice it as a change in state, colour or temperature, or a gas being given off. We write the change as a chemical equation and then balance it, because atoms are only rearranged, never created or destroyed. Almost every reaction in this chapter sorts into one of five types, and many are also redox reactions - oxygen or hydrogen changing hands. Most marks sit in writing the correct balanced equation with the right classification.

Formula sheet

  • Combination A + B -> AB (two or more reactants -> a single product)
  • Decomposition AB -> A + B (one reactant -> two or more products)
  • Displacement A + BC -> AC + B (A more reactive than B)
  • Double displacement AB + CD -> AD + CB (ions exchanged)
  • Oxidation / Reduction Oxidation: gain O or lose H . Reduction: lose O or gain H
  • Conservation of mass atoms of each element (reactants) = atoms of each element (products)
  • Everyday oxidation Corrosion (rusting of iron) . Rancidity (fats/oils oxidise) - slowed by antioxidants / nitrogen flush

Key definitions

Balanced chemical equation
The same number of atoms of every element on the left and the right of the arrow - it must obey the law of conservation of mass. NCERT 2026-27 Ch1 p.14
Combination reaction
Two or more reactants join to give a single product, e.g. calcium oxide + water gives slaked lime. NCERT 2026-27 Ch1 p.6
Decomposition reaction
The opposite of combination - one reactant breaks down into two or more simpler products, usually needing heat, light or electricity. NCERT 2026-27 Ch1 p.8
Displacement reaction
A more reactive element pushes a less reactive one out of its compound - iron displaces copper from copper sulphate solution. NCERT 2026-27 Ch1 p.14
Double displacement reaction
The two reactants swap their ions/partners; an insoluble product often drops out as a precipitate. NCERT 2026-27 Ch1 p.12
Oxidation and reduction
Oxidation adds oxygen (or removes hydrogen); reduction removes oxygen (or adds hydrogen). When one substance is oxidised and the other reduced in the same reaction it is a redox reaction. NCERT 2026-27 Ch1 p.13
Redox reaction (oxidation-reduction)
A reaction in which one substance is oxidised and, at the same time, another substance is reduced. NCERT 2026-27 Ch1 p.12
Exothermic reaction
A reaction that gives out heat to the surroundings - respiration, combustion, and quicklime added to water are examples. NCERT 2026-27 Ch1 p.7
Endothermic reaction
A reaction that takes in energy from the surroundings - most decomposition reactions are endothermic. NCERT 2026-27 Ch1 p.10
Corrosion
The slow eating-away of a metal by air, moisture or acids - the rusting of iron is the everyday example. NCERT 2026-27 Ch1 p.13
Rancidity
The oxidation of fats and oils in food, which spoils their smell and taste; slowed by antioxidants or by flushing the pack with nitrogen. NCERT 2026-27 Ch1 p.13
Skeletal chemical equation
Such a chemical equation is a skeletal chemical equation for a reaction. NCERT 2026-27 Ch1 p.3
Precipitation reaction
Any reaction that produces a precipitate can be called a precipitation reaction. NCERT 2026-27 Ch1 p.11

What the board actually asks

balance a skeletal equation and add the physical-state symbols (s)/(l)/(g)/(aq); identify the reaction type (combination, decomposition, displacement, double displacement/precipitation, or oxidation-reduction); classify a decomposition by its energy source (heat, light or electricity); say which substance is oxidised and which is reduced; and explain the everyday effects of oxidation - corrosion and rancidity. The balanced equation IS the answer in most questions here.

Common mistakes — where marks are lost

  • Changing a formula to balance an equation - e.g. writing H2O4 instead of putting a 4 before H2O. Only the coefficients may be changed, never the subscripts inside a formula.
  • Leaving out the state symbols (s)/(l)/(g)/(aq) when the question asks for a complete or a balanced-with-states equation.
  • Confusing displacement (a single element is displaced) with double displacement (ions are exchanged between two compounds).
  • Reversing oxidation and reduction. Remember it by the oxygen: gain of oxygen is oxidation, loss of oxygen is reduction.
  • Calling a precipitation reaction a combination reaction. A white insoluble product means a double displacement (precipitation) reaction, not a combination.
